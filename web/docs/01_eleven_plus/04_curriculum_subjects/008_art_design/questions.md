# Answer Keys and Explanations

## 1. Comparing Artworks

1. **Similar style and technique:** Look for similar brushstrokes, colour choices, handling of light and arrangement of objects. These provide stronger evidence of a shared style than subject alone.
2. **Similar brushstrokes:** The artists may use a similar technique even though they choose different colours.
3. **Different approaches to detail:** Careful lines create a precise appearance, while broad, loose marks produce a more expressive or impressionistic effect.
4. **Similar visual techniques:** Similar handling of colour, light, brushstrokes or composition provides evidence of a shared artistic approach.
5. **The pair with the most shared visual features:** Similar brushwork, colour relationships and composition provide stronger evidence than simply showing the sea.
6. **Different composition:** Changing the positions of objects changes the balance, focal point and overall impression of a picture.
7. **Different technique or composition:** Line, brushwork, texture, proportions and the arrangement of objects can make pictures look very different even when their colours match.
8. **The same subject is insufficient evidence:** Many artists paint the same subjects, so technique, style and visual details are more useful for comparison.
9. **Lines suggesting movement:** Curved, diagonal or repeated lines and the positions of figures can help show movement.
10. **Different colours, lighting or composition:** Darker colours, stronger contrasts or a more dramatic arrangement can make a scene feel threatening rather than calm.
11. **Similar techniques:** Similar brushstrokes and ways of applying colour are generally stronger evidence of a shared style than simply depicting similar subjects.
12. **Different visual treatment:** Changing the colours, composition and marks can produce a different mood, emphasis and artistic interpretation.

## 2. Identifying Artistic Styles from Images

1. **The most detailed and accurately proportioned image:** Realistic art aims to represent subjects as they appear in real life.
2. **The image that changes or simplifies real objects:** This suggests a less realistic approach, potentially including abstraction.
3. **The image that uses recognisable objects as repeated decorative elements:** Objects can be arranged to create a pattern rather than represent a realistic scene.
4. **The image using basic shapes to represent objects:** Simplification reduces detail and can create a stylised appearance.
5. **The image with the most carefully observed details:** Accurate textures, proportions, shadows and small features support realism.
6. **The image that focuses on shapes, colours and forms rather than recognisable subjects:** This is characteristic of abstract art.
7. **Exaggerated facial features change the representation:** They may emphasise personality, emotion or a particular feature rather than realistic proportions.
8. **The picture with accurate proportions and observed details:** These features suggest that the artist aims to represent an actual scene faithfully.
9. **The image where shapes and colours dominate:** Abstract art does not necessarily depend on recognisable objects.
10. **The artist is deliberately altering reality:** Changed proportions may emphasise an idea, emotion or artistic style.
11. **The least recognisable or most simplified image:** The picture that departs most from the appearance of its real subject is the least realistic.
12. **Compare representation and technique:** Look at the level of detail, recognisable forms, colour treatment and degree of simplification to distinguish the approaches.

## 3. Colour and Visual Effects

1. **The painting dominated by reds, oranges and yellows:** These are commonly associated with warmth.
2. **Cool blues, blue-greens and muted whites:** These colours can suggest cold temperatures and a winter atmosphere.
3. **Different emotional effects:** Bright colours often appear lively, while muted colours can feel subdued or subdued in intensity.
4. **A contrasting colour against the background:** Strong colour contrast makes an object easier to notice.
5. **A scheme with strong contrasts and rich sunset colours:** Reds, oranges and purples can create a dramatic sunset effect.
6. **The picture with the strongest differences between colours:** Strong contrast makes neighbouring colours appear more distinct.
7. **A limited palette creates unity:** Using fewer colours can make a painting feel harmonious and help establish a consistent mood.
8. **The scheme with strong contrast between text and background:** This improves readability and makes the message easier to notice.
9. **The image with bright, lively colours arranged effectively:** Bright colours can help communicate cheerfulness, although composition also influences mood.
10. **A warmer and potentially more energetic atmosphere:** Reds and oranges can feel warmer than blue, although the overall effect depends on the other colours.
11. **A contrasting colour combination:** An object is easier to distinguish when its colour differs clearly from its surroundings.
12. **Natural, muted greens, browns and other earthy colours:** These often support a natural or calming appearance.
13. **The image with the clearest colour separation:** Contrasting the subject with its background helps the subject stand out.
14. **Different composition or distribution of colours:** Balance depends on the placement, size and visual weight of colour areas, not just their brightness.
15. **The arrangement with the strongest colour emphasis:** A distinctive colour on the main object can draw attention to it.

## 4. Light, Shade and Three-Dimensional Form

1. **The sphere with gradual shading and a highlight:** Gradual tonal changes make a flat circle appear rounded.
2. **The cube with consistent highlights and shadows:** The light and dark areas should match a single, believable light source.
3. **Add highlights and graduated shading:** These help show the curved surface and the direction of the light.
4. **The picture with the greatest tonal contrast:** Strong differences between light and dark areas create a more dramatic tonal effect.
5. **The portrait with convincing changes in tone:** Shading around the cheeks, nose, eyes and jaw helps create facial depth.
6. **The shadow touching the object and extending across the supporting surface:** This helps anchor the object in its surroundings.
7. **Light is most likely coming from the right:** A shadow usually falls in the direction opposite to the light source.
8. **The image with sharp highlights and strong reflections:** These are common visual clues of a shiny metal surface.
9. **Use subtle tonal differences around the cup:** Gentle shadows and highlights separate the white cup from its background.
10. **The image with bright highlights and sharp changes in tone:** These can make a surface appear glossy or reflective.
11. **The first ball has three-dimensional shading:** Highlights and gradual shadows suggest a curved surface, while a flat circle lacks this tonal information.
12. **The image using perspective and tonal contrast to separate nearer and farther objects:** These features can help create depth.
13. **The shadow contradicts the light source:** If the light comes from one side, the shadow should generally extend away from it.
14. **The drawing with gradual darkening away from the light:** This creates the impression of a curved surface.
15. **The image with selective lighting and deep shadows:** Strong contrasts and partially hidden details can create mystery.

## 5. Lines, Shapes, Patterns and Texture

1. **The drawing with irregular, broken and varied marks:** These suggest the uneven surface of tree bark.
2. **The picture with consistently repeated shapes and spacing:** Repetition makes the pattern look regular.
3. **The design with a clearly repeated unit:** Repeating the same arrangement at consistent intervals creates a recognisable pattern.
4. **Curved, flowing lines:** These can suggest the movement of water.
5. **The image using directional or repeated lines:** Diagonal and curved marks can suggest movement and direction.
6. **The drawing with smooth, even transitions and few rough marks:** These help represent smooth fabric.
7. **The design using regular geometric forms:** Repeated circles, squares, triangles or other regular shapes create geometric consistency.
8. **The picture containing the greatest range of distinct shapes:** A greater variety of shapes creates more visual diversity.
9. **The arrangement with irregular, intersecting lines and uneven shapes:** This can create a chaotic or unsettled impression.
10. **The image with consistent repetition and spacing:** Regular intervals make the pattern appear orderly.
11. **The different shape becomes a focal point:** Breaking a pattern draws attention to the unusual element.
12. **The drawing with varied short marks and irregular shading:** These can imitate the uneven surface of stone.
13. **Different spacing, size or distribution:** Uneven spacing or a concentration of shapes on one side can make a pattern feel unbalanced.
14. **The drawing with stronger or thicker foreground lines and lighter background lines:** This helps distinguish nearer objects from more distant ones.
15. **The image with the clearest use of both straight and curved lines:** Their contrasting qualities make the difference easy to see.
16. **Soft, fine and overlapping marks:** These suggest the delicate, layered appearance of feathers.
17. **The design whose two halves mirror one another:** Matching shapes and positions on either side of an axis indicate symmetry.
18. **Unequal spacing or deliberately varied elements:** These distinguish an irregular pattern from one that repeats uniformly.

## 6. Composition and Arrangement

1. **The image with the clearest main subject:** Contrast, size, placement or detail can establish a focal point.
2. **The image where the subject is placed to support the intended effect:** Effective placement guides the viewer's attention and suits the purpose of the picture.
3. **Different visual balance and spacing:** Grouping objects can make a scene feel concentrated, while spreading them out can create openness.
4. **The landscape with overlapping forms, smaller distant objects and a clear foreground:** These features help create depth.
5. **Place the bird where it stands out clearly:** A contrasting colour, prominent position or surrounding empty space can attract attention.
6. **The image with well-distributed visual weight:** Balance can be achieved through the size, colour, position and number of objects.
7. **The image showing more of the room and using perspective convincingly:** Visible floor and wall lines can help suggest a larger space.
