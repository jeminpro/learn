# FSCE Gloucestershire Physical Education Teaching Pack

A parent and child guide to the physical education points named in the FSCE Gloucestershire parent guide, through Year 5.

The official guide identifies five practical areas: running, jumping, throwing and catching; attacking and defending in competitive games; flexibility, strength, technique, control and balance; comparing performance and improving; and physical confidence, health, fitness, fairness and respect. This edition includes only those areas. The child familiarisation guide's sprint question also illustrates coordination.

The guide says this is **not a practical sporting ability test**. Oak National Academy lessons linked below are examples for teaching the FSCE-listed ideas. Their sport names and lesson sequences are not an FSCE list of examinable sports or facts.

## How to use the pack

1. Read one topic with your child, demonstrate it in a safe space, and ask why one choice works better in a particular situation.
2. Use a soft ball and simple games. Observe one useful action and one improvement goal.
3. Practise explaining answers in a sentence; the FSCE sample asks children to identify the most relevant quality in a straight sprint.

## 1 Movement skills

**Learn** Recognise and combine basic movements and explain how a change in technique can help.

### Teach it

Running uses repeated steps to move quickly or steadily. In a sprint, a controlled arm swing, forward focus and coordinated arms and legs help movement. A sprint is a short, fast run; controlled arms and legs help the runner move efficiently.

Jumping includes taking off, travelling and landing. A jump can start on one foot or two; a hop takes off and lands on the same foot. A leap usually travels from one foot to the other. Bend the knees on landing and keep balance. Compare height, distance and controlled landing rather than calling every large jump “better”.

Throwing sends an object away using the body and an arm; the direction, release and amount of force influence its path. Catching involves looking at the object, moving into its path and receiving it with controlled hands. Practise underarm and overarm throws with a soft ball. Aiming accurately and throwing far are different goals.

Skills can be isolated (catch while standing) or combined (run into space, catch, turn and pass). Discuss which action is needed at each step.

### Words and concepts

**Sprint** A short race run as fast as possible.

**Coordination** Body parts working together in the right order and at the right time.

**Take off and landing** The start and end of a jump.

**Accuracy** How close a throw or movement comes to its intended target.

**Force** A push or pull; more force may send a ball farther but can reduce accuracy.

### Try it

Make three stations: a ten-step run, a standing jump and five gentle throws at a target. Describe which body parts work together and what changes when you link a run to a throw. Keep scores only to compare your own attempts.

### Check understanding

**Ask** Why might coordination matter more than tactics in a straight sprint with no opponents to dodge?

**Answer** Running efficiently depends on arms and legs working together; complex attacking tactics have little role in a straight individual sprint.

**Ask** A child can throw far but misses a small target. What should they practise?

**Answer** Direction and accuracy, possibly using a controlled amount of force.

**Ask** Name a combined movement.

**Answer** For example, run to a free space, catch a ball, turn and pass.

### Correct a common mistake

Jumping farther is not automatically the best attempt if the child falls on landing. A controlled, safe landing is part of technique.

## 2 Physical qualities and body control

**Learn** Tell apart qualities of movement and select the most relevant one for a situation.

### Teach it

Balance means maintaining a steady body position while still or moving. Standing on one foot tests still balance; moving along a line tests moving balance. A wider base of support often makes a position steadier.

Strength is the ability to exert force. Flexibility is the available range of movement around a joint. Control means choosing and regulating body position, speed and force. Technique is the way a skill is performed. Good technique varies by activity and should support both the goal and safety.

Coordination means body parts work together. The FSCE sprint example makes it relevant even though the parent guide lists the five qualities above. A safe landing can involve strength, balance and control at the same time.

Physical confidence and fitness develop through varied, age appropriate activity. One strong quality does not prove every physical quality is equally strong.

### Words and concepts

**Balance** Staying steady while still or in motion.

**Base of support** The area beneath the body that supports it; feet farther apart often increase stability.

**Strength** Ability to produce force with muscles.

**Flexibility** Ability to move a joint through a range of motion.

**Technique** The method used to perform a movement.

**Control** Regulating movement and body position.

### Try it

Try one-foot balance, heel-to-toe walking, wall push-ups and a gentle reach. Ask which quality each activity tests most strongly and why. Swap the surface or speed only if safe.

### Check understanding

**Ask** A pupil falls off a narrow balance line. Which quality is most directly involved?

**Answer** Balance, supported by control and coordination.

**Ask** Which quality is relevant to reaching comfortably through a movement?

**Answer** Flexibility.

**Ask** Does a fast sprint prove a child has excellent balance?

**Answer** No. Speed and balance describe different aspects of movement.

### Correct a common mistake

Words can overlap, but they are not interchangeable. Ask which quality is most important in the stated situation rather than claiming only one quality ever matters.

## 3 Attacking and defending

**Learn** Explain simple choices that help a team gain or prevent a scoring chance.

### Teach it

An attacker attempts to keep possession, move towards a target or create a chance to score. A defender attempts to delay, block a path, mark an attacker or regain possession within the rules. Roles change when possession changes.

Space is an area an attacker can move into. An attacker can spread out, make a run, pass to an unmarked teammate or change direction. Support is moving into a place where a teammate can pass safely. Moving without the ball can be as useful as moving with it.

A defender can mark an opponent, stay between attacker and target, cover a space, intercept a pass, or delay a player until teammates recover. Fair defence depends on the particular game: reaching for a pass can be fine, but pushing and deliberate tripping are not acceptable.

A tactic is a planned choice to help a goal; a skill is the action used to carry it out. Passing is a skill; passing into empty space to avoid a defender is a tactic. Choose the simplest useful decision for the situation.

### Words and concepts

**Attack and defence** Trying to score or progress; trying to prevent that progress.

**Possession** Having control of the ball or playing object.

**Space** An open area useful for movement or a pass.

**Mark** Stay close enough to limit an opponent’s choices.

**Intercept** Stop or take a pass on its way to a teammate.

**Cover** Protect a teammate’s position or an open area.

**Support** Provide a teammate with a useful passing option.

**Tactic** A choice or plan for a game situation.

**Transition** The moment a team changes from attacking to defending or back.

### Try it

Play two attackers against one defender using a soft ball and two cone goals. Pause before each pass and ask the passer to point to a useful space. Rotate positions and ask why the defender chose a particular place to stand.

### Check understanding

**Ask** Your teammate has the ball and is closely marked. What can you do?

**Answer** Move into an open passing space so they have an option.

**Ask** What should a defender do if an attacker is between them and the goal?

**Answer** Move to protect the route to the goal while following the game rules.

**Ask** When a team loses the ball, what changes?

**Answer** It moves from attacking to defending and tries to protect space or regain possession.

### Correct a common mistake

“Marking” does not mean grabbing or pushing a player. The exact allowed contact depends on the game; fair play always matters.

## 4 Health and fitness

**Learn** Connect regular activity with physical confidence, fitness and healthy participation.

### Teach it

Regular movement can help a child practise skills, build confidence and enjoy being active. Different activities call for different qualities: a gentle push can show strength, a comfortable reach can show flexibility, and standing steadily can show balance.

A gentle start can prepare a child for an activity. Ask how movement changes confidence and the ability to keep taking part.

Breathing and heartbeat often become faster during active play. A child need only connect active participation with health and fitness; detailed body systems are outside the FSCE PE list in the parent guide.

Being physically confident means feeling able to participate, try a skill and improve. Winning is not required.

### Words and concepts

**Active** Moving the body rather than staying still.

**Fitness** Ability to cope with different physical activity demands.

**Confidence** Willingness to take part and try a skill.

**Safety** Reducing avoidable risk to yourself and others.

### Try it

After a walk and a more active game, ask how the body feels and how repeated participation may build confidence and fitness.

### Check understanding

**Ask** Why begin a game with easier movement?

**Answer** It lets the body and attention prepare gradually for the activity.

**Ask** Which shows a child taking part for longer: one jump or a sustained active game?

**Answer** The sustained active game.

**Ask** Must the best athlete win to show improvement?

**Answer** No. A child can improve their own performance even if another player is faster.

### Correct a common mistake

The official PE list does not specify fitness targets or physiology facts. Focus on sensible links between activity, confidence, health and fitness.

## 5 Fairness and respect in competition

**Learn** Identify choices that include others and keep competition fair.

### Teach it

Working with others can help a child participate fairly: listen, take turns, follow the agreed rules and include teammates.

Fairness means following the agreed rules consistently and giving others a reasonable chance to participate. Respect includes listening to the official, recognising good play, avoiding insults and responding appropriately when winning or losing.

Playing without unsafe contact and acknowledging an opponent's effort are further examples of respect.

Competition against others and competition with a previous personal result are both useful. Winning is an outcome; improvement and conduct are separate things worth noticing.

### Words and concepts

**Cooperation** Working with others on a shared task.

**Fair play** Following agreed rules and treating others fairly.

**Respect** Taking other people and their role seriously.

**Sportsmanship** Honest and considerate conduct during a game.

**Inclusion** Making room for different participants to join in.

**Competition** An activity in which participants try to achieve a result, alone or against others.

### Try it

Play a passing game where a team scores only after every teammate has received the ball. Discuss how the new rule changes teamwork. Afterwards each player names one fair decision and one helpful communication choice.

### Check understanding

**Ask** Your team is winning and a teammate has not touched the ball. What is a good choice?

**Answer** Look for a safe opportunity to include them while still following the game rules.

**Ask** You think an official made a wrong decision. What can you do?

**Answer** Respond respectfully and continue; ask calmly for clarification when appropriate.

**Ask** Is a win enough to prove a team played fairly?

**Answer** No. The team also needs to follow rules and respect others.

### Correct a common mistake

“Fair” does not always mean every child produces the same result. Fair participation can involve appropriate adaptations to the activity.

## 6 Comparing and improving performance

**Learn** Use observations and simple evidence to set one practical improvement goal.

### Teach it

Performance is how a skill or activity was carried out on a particular attempt. Evaluate by identifying something that worked and one change to try. Choose a criterion that matches the goal: distance for a long jump, accuracy for a target throw, controlled balance for a hold, or clear timing for a movement sequence.

For a fair comparison, try to keep equipment, distance, task and rules the same. Record date, activity, result and a note about technique. A personal best is the child’s strongest result in that task so far; the next attempt may still be weaker because results vary.

Feedback should be specific and actionable: “look at the target before releasing” gives a possible change; “do better” does not. Practise one adjustment, repeat safely and compare again. Progress may show in better control or technique even before time or distance changes.

Observing a partner can help when the observer knows what to watch for. A short video may help discuss body position, but ask permission before recording someone and keep feedback kind.

### Words and concepts

**Performance** What someone did on an attempt.

**Evaluate** Judge what worked using evidence.

**Criterion** The feature used to judge or compare.

**Personal best** The best previous result for that person in the same activity.

**Technique goal** A specific change to how the movement is done.

**Feedback** Useful information about what went well or what to adjust.

**Repeat** Try the same activity again to compare fairly.

**Measure** Record a count, distance, time or other relevant result.

### Try it

Try three target throws from the same marked line. Record hits out of five, choose one adjustment and repeat. Discuss whether the comparison is fair if the target or line moves.

### Check understanding

**Ask** A child throws from three metres, then four metres. Can you directly compare hit rates?

**Answer** Not as a like-for-like test; the distance changed.

**Ask** Which comment is more useful, “be better” or “aim at the centre before releasing”?

**Answer** The second gives a clear action.

**Ask** A child does not beat their best score today. Have they necessarily lost skill?

**Answer** No. Performance varies; look at technique and several attempts.

### Correct a common mistake

Improvement is not restricted to a faster time or higher score. More control, consistency, safe technique or teamwork can also show progress.

### Teaching the overlap

**Movement and technique.** A child runs a short marked distance twice. Ask what the arms and legs did and whether the finish point was the same. The useful idea is coordinated movement and improving technique, not specialist athletics rules.

**Attack and defence.** In a simple cone-goal game, an attacker moves into open space. The defender stays between the attacker and the goal without pushing. Pause, swap roles and ask what each player is trying to achieve.

**Strength, flexibility and balance.** Try a wall push, a comfortable reach and a one-leg stand. Ask which named quality each mostly demonstrates. Explain that control and technique help with each activity.

**Improvement.** Record three standing jumps from the same line, note the landing as well as the distance, and choose a small adjustment for the next try. Comparing the child's own attempts reflects the FSCE guide's example.

**Fairness and respect.** After a competitive game, ask what rule the child followed, how they treated the other player, and what they could do if an opponent made a good move.

## FSCE aligned practice questions

1. What is the difference between throwing for distance and throwing at a target?
2. Name two movements you could combine while playing with a ball.
3. Why can bending the knees help a child land a jump with control?
4. In an individual straight sprint, which is more relevant: coordination or a plan to mark an opponent? Explain.
5. What does balance mean when standing on one leg?
6. What quality helps a child reach comfortably through a movement?
7. Give one example of strength used during a simple activity.
8. A defender stands between an attacker and the goal. What are they trying to prevent?
9. How can an attacker use open space?
10. What should a player do when their team loses the ball?
11. Is pushing an opponent a fair way to mark them?
12. A child runs the same short route twice. What must stay the same for a fair comparison?
13. Which feedback is useful: “do better” or “look ahead and swing your arms in rhythm”? Why?
14. Does a player have to win to show improvement or respect?
15. Name one way a child can demonstrate fairness after a game.

### Answer guide

1. One seeks greater distance; the other seeks accuracy at the intended target.
2. For example, run then catch, or catch then turn and throw.
3. A controlled knee bend can help the body absorb the landing and remain steady.
4. Coordination, because arms and legs work together during the run; there is no opponent to mark.
5. Keeping the body steady over the supporting foot.
6. Flexibility.
7. For example, pushing gently against a wall or making a controlled jump.
8. A route to the goal or a scoring chance.
9. Move into it to receive a pass or create a scoring chance.
10. Change from attacking to defending and help protect useful space.
11. No. Marking must follow the rules and show respect for the opponent.
12. The distance, start and finish points, equipment and basic task.
13. The second: it suggests a particular adjustment to technique.
14. No. Improvement can be measured against a previous attempt; respect concerns conduct.
15. Follow a rule, accept the result or acknowledge an opponent's good play.
