# PE Questions for Discussion with Answers

These 96 questions invite a child to explain an idea, give an example, and consider what might change in a different situation. The suggested answers are models, not the only acceptable wording. Questions about health, diet, injuries and school PE go beyond the specific PE points named in the FSCE Gloucestershire parent guide; use them for general discussion.

## 1. Health and Fitness

1. Why is warming up important before exercise?
2. What would happen if you never exercised?
3. How does exercise make your body stronger?
4. Why does your heart beat faster during exercise?
5. What happens to muscles when you train regularly?
6. Why is rest important after exercise?
7. What would happen if someone only did one type of exercise?
8. How does breathing change during activity?
9. Why is fitness important in daily life?
10. What would happen if diet and exercise were both poor?
11. Which is more important: strength or endurance? Why?
12. Should children exercise every day? Why?

### Suggested answers

1. A gentle warm-up lets the body and mind prepare for harder movement. For example, walking and then jogging is a more gradual start than suddenly sprinting. It may reduce the chance of injury, but it cannot prevent every injury.
2. Without regular physical activity, a person may find active tasks harder and miss chances to practise movement skills. Sitting less and adding enjoyable movement is a sensible way to begin; one missed day is not a problem.
3. When muscles work against a suitable challenge and have time to recover, they can adapt so the same task becomes easier. Running, climbing and age-appropriate pushing or pulling all challenge the body in different ways.
4. Working muscles need more oxygen and energy. The heart pumps blood faster to help deliver these and carry away waste products.
5. With suitable practice and recovery, muscles can become better at the tasks they perform. The change depends on the activity: practising a jump and practising a long walk challenge the body differently.
6. Rest gives the body time to recover after effort and helps a child feel ready to play again. Muscles and energy levels need recovery, especially after a demanding activity.
7. They might improve at that activity but miss chances to practise other movement skills and qualities. A mixture of enjoyable activities can develop strength, stamina, flexibility, balance and coordination.
8. Breathing usually becomes faster and deeper as activity becomes harder because the body needs more oxygen. It gradually settles as the effort eases.
9. Fitness can make everyday tasks such as walking, climbing stairs, carrying appropriate items and playing feel easier. It also helps people take part in activities they enjoy.
10. Over time, a pattern of little activity and an unbalanced diet could make it harder to support health and fitness. One meal or one quiet day does not decide a person's health; habits over time matter.
11. Neither is always more important. Strength helps with a forceful task such as lifting an appropriate object; endurance helps someone keep walking or playing for longer. The answer depends on what the person needs to do.
12. Children should have opportunities to be active regularly through play, walking, games and other suitable activities. UK guidance for ages 5–18 recommends **an average of at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity each day across the week**, with a variety of activities. That is an average, not a requirement for a formal workout every day.

## 2. Movement and Body Control

1. Why is balance important in sports?
2. What happens if coordination is poor?
3. How does practice improve movement?
4. Why do athletes repeat movements?
5. What would happen if you couldn’t control your body well?
6. How does agility help in games?
7. Why is timing important in movement?
8. What happens if you move too slowly in sport?
9. How do different sports use different movements?
10. Why is posture important?
11. Which is harder: running or balancing? Why?
12. Should all sports require the same skills? Why?

### Suggested answers

1. Balance helps a player remain steady while stopping, turning, landing or reaching. A jumper, for example, needs to regain balance after landing.
2. Movements may be less smooth or accurate when body parts do not work well together. Practice can help: in a sprint, coordinated arm and leg movements make running more controlled.
3. Repeated attempts help a child notice what works and adjust it. Practising a catch with a suitable ball can help the child track the ball and position their hands.
4. Repetition gives them chances to improve the same technique and use it reliably. They should still check whether they are practising a useful movement, rather than simply repeating a mistake.
5. They might find it harder to stop, land, turn or send a ball where intended. Beginning with an easier movement and building control gradually can help.
6. Agility helps a player change direction quickly while staying controlled, such as moving away from a defender. Speed without control might send the player into a crowded area.
7. A catch, pass or jump works best when it happens at the right moment. Hands that close before a ball arrives, for example, may miss it.
8. In some situations an opponent may reach a space or ball first. In others, slowing down is the better decision because it improves control or safety. The correct speed depends on the task.
9. A game might combine running, catching and throwing, while another uses careful balance and controlled landing. Many basic movements can be used in more than one activity.
10. Body position can help with balance and technique. For example, looking ahead and staying steady can help a child walk along a marked line.
11. It depends on the person and the exact task. A short run may feel easy to one child, while a long one-foot balance is harder; another child may find the opposite. Explain what makes each task challenging.
12. No. Activities use different combinations of running, jumping, throwing, catching, balance and control. Some skills transfer between activities, but the demands are not identical.

## 3. Rules and Tactics

1. Why do sports have rules?
2. What would happen if there were no rules in games?
3. How do rules make games fair?
4. Why are tactics important in winning games?
5. What happens if a team ignores strategy?
6. How do players decide what move to make?
7. Why do different sports need different rules?
8. What would happen if everyone played individually in team sports?
9. How do rules change the way we play?
10. Why is fairness important in sport?
11. Which is more important: skill or tactics? Why?
12. Should rules ever change? Why?

### Suggested answers

1. Rules tell everyone the aim, what actions are allowed, how to score and how to play safely. Shared rules let opponents take part in the same game.
2. Players might disagree about goals, boundaries and fair actions. A simple game still needs agreed rules, even if the players invent them themselves.
3. They give participants the same expectations and let decisions be made consistently. A rule helps only when everyone understands and follows it.
4. A tactic is a useful plan for a situation, such as moving into space to receive a pass or marking an attacker. Good tactics can create chances, but they do not guarantee a win.
5. Players may bunch together, leave useful space unguarded or miss passing options. A simple shared plan can help them work together.
6. They look at the goal, teammates, opponents, available space and the rules, then choose a useful action. Sometimes a safe pass is better than trying to move past several defenders.
7. Activities have different aims, equipment and ways of moving. Rules describe how each game works and help keep it fair and safe.
8. Teammates might have fewer passing options and leave areas unprotected. Sharing the ball and covering different spaces can improve the team's play.
9. A rule can limit or allow an action, changing the choices available. If a game allows only three steps with the ball, for example, a player may need to pass sooner.
10. Fairness lets everyone compete with shared expectations and respect. A victory gained by breaking an agreed rule is not fair play.
11. Both matter in different ways. A player needs a skill to make a pass, but also a tactic to decide **when and where** to pass it.
12. Yes, when everyone agrees before play or a teacher adapts a game for safety, learning or inclusion. Changing a rule secretly during a game would be unfair.

## 4. Teamwork and Decision Making

1. Why is teamwork important in sport?
2. What would happen if a team didn’t communicate?
3. How does cooperation improve performance?
4. Why do teams assign different roles?
5. What happens if one player dominates the team?
6. How do players make quick decisions?
7. Why is trust important in teams?
8. What would happen if no one listened to instructions?
9. How do teams recover from mistakes?
10. Why is leadership important in sport?
11. Which is better: strong individual or strong team? Why?
12. Should everyone in a team have equal roles? Why?

### Suggested answers

1. Team members can use different positions and strengths to reach a shared goal. One player might move into space while another makes a pass.
2. Players might make the same move, miss a pass or leave an attacker unmarked. Short, clear calls and listening can help.
3. Teammates can offer passing options, cover spaces and encourage one another. Cooperation can give a team more choices than one player has alone.
4. Different roles help cover the tasks in a game, such as attacking, defending or supporting. Roles can change as possession or the situation changes.
5. Others may have fewer chances to practise and contribute, and the team might become predictable. Sharing decisions and opportunities can help.
6. They notice the most useful information, such as open space, a teammate and a defender, and choose an action they have practised. A decision can be changed if the situation changes.
7. Trust helps players rely on teammates to follow rules, offer support and try their roles. It grows through honest communication and respectful behaviour.
8. Players might misunderstand the task, miss a rule or act unsafely. Listening, then asking for clarification when needed, helps everyone take part.
9. They acknowledge the mistake, encourage the player and choose a better next action. Blaming someone usually makes cooperation harder.
10. A helpful leader can listen, organise a plan and encourage everyone. Leadership is not the same as shouting or doing all the work.
11. It depends on the situation, but a team game usually benefits when several players cooperate and use their skills. One strong individual may create a chance; the team still needs to support it.
12. Everyone should have a fair chance to contribute and be respected. They do not all need identical jobs at every moment; roles can suit the task and be rotated when possible.

## 5. Training and Improvement

1. Why do athletes train regularly?
2. What happens if you don’t practise?
3. How does repetition improve performance?
4. Why do athletes set goals?
5. What would happen if training was random?
6. How do you measure improvement?
7. Why is feedback important in sport?
8. What happens if you push too hard in training?
9. How do athletes improve weaknesses?
10. Why is consistency important?
11. Which is more important: effort or talent? Why?
12. Should training always be difficult? Why?

### Suggested answers

1. Regular practice gives them repeated chances to improve a skill, strength or stamina and use it more confidently. Rest is also part of a sensible routine.
2. You may miss chances to learn a new skill or improve one you already have. People can still return to practice later; progress is not lost forever after a short break.
3. Repetition lets you notice an error, try an adjustment and make a useful movement more reliable. Repeating a movement without paying attention may not improve it much.
4. A clear goal tells them what to practise and how to recognise progress. “Land the next three jumps steadily” is clearer than “be good at jumping.”
5. They might spend time on unrelated tasks and find it harder to see whether a particular skill improved. Variety is useful, but a goal gives practice direction.
6. Repeat the same task under similar conditions and record a relevant result: time, distance, accurate throws or quality of a controlled landing. Compare it with an earlier attempt by the same child.
7. Useful feedback tells someone what worked and gives one change to try. “Look at the target before throwing” is more helpful than “do better.”
8. They could become overly tired, lose good technique or increase their risk of injury. A child should build activity gradually and tell an adult about pain or feeling unwell.
9. They identify one skill that needs work, practise a manageable version, ask for feedback and try again. A weakness today can become a stronger skill through practice.
10. Returning to a skill regularly gives more chances to apply feedback and notice progress. It does not mean repeating the hardest activity every day.
11. Natural ability may help someone begin, but effort, useful practice, support and opportunities all affect progress. A child can control their effort and learning choices more than their starting ability.
12. No. Easier practice can teach technique or rebuild confidence; harder challenges can be added when the child is ready. Safe, enjoyable activity and recovery matter too.

## 6. Safety and Injury Prevention

1. Why is safety important in sport?
2. What would happen if people didn’t warm up?
3. How do injuries happen?
4. Why is correct equipment important?
5. What happens if rules are ignored?
6. How can injuries be prevented?
7. Why do athletes stretch?
8. What would happen if surfaces were unsafe?
9. How does technique reduce injury?
10. Why is hydration important?
11. Which is safer: indoor or outdoor sport? Why?
12. Should safety always come before performance? Why?

### Suggested answers

1. Safety helps people participate without avoidable harm. Checking space, equipment, rules and how others are moving is part of playing well.
2. They might feel less prepared for a demanding movement, and a sudden hard effort may increase injury risk. Missing a warm-up does **not** mean an injury is certain.
3. Injuries can happen through falls, collisions, unsuitable equipment, unsafe surfaces, too much effort or poor technique. Sometimes an accident happens despite sensible care.
4. Suitable equipment fits the activity and the player. A soft ball, for example, can make a beginner's catching game easier and safer.
5. Players might collide, argue or gain an unfair advantage. Some rules exist specifically to protect players.
6. Use suitable equipment and space, learn the rules, start gradually, practise controlled technique and stop if something hurts. These steps lower risk but cannot prevent every injury.
7. Gentle stretching can help someone explore and maintain a comfortable range of movement. It should not be forced or painful, and stretching alone does not guarantee that injuries will not happen.
8. A slippery, uneven or cluttered surface could increase the chance of a slip, trip or fall. Check the area and choose another place or adapt the game if needed.
9. Controlled technique can help a player move or land in a steadier way. For instance, bending the knees and regaining balance after a jump may reduce a hard, awkward landing.
10. Fluid helps the body function during activity, especially when a child is hot or active for longer. Have drinks available and respond to thirst; the amount needed depends on the person and conditions.
11. Neither is always safer. An indoor floor can be slippery; an outdoor field can be uneven. Check the actual surface, space, weather, equipment and supervision.
12. Yes. A faster result is not worth an avoidable injury to yourself or someone else. Choose a safer version of the task if the setting or equipment is unsuitable.

## 7. Comparison and Evaluation

1. Which is better: team sports or individual sports? Why?
2. Is strength or speed more important?
3. Which is harder: football or athletics? Why?
4. Should winning matter more than participation?
5. Which is more important: practice or natural ability?
6. Is indoor or outdoor sport better? Why?
7. Which improves fitness more: running or swimming?
8. Should all sports have competition? Why?
9. Which is more fun: structured or free play?
10. Is sport more physical or mental? Why?
11. Which sport requires more teamwork? Why?
12. Should PE be graded in school? Why?

### Suggested answers

1. Neither is better for everyone. Team sports give chances to cooperate; individual sports let someone focus on their own performance. Give an example of what a particular child enjoys or wants to learn.
2. It depends on the task. Strength helps with producing force; speed helps with moving quickly. A child might need both for some movements.
3. It depends on the activity within each sport and the child doing it. Football can involve fast decisions with opponents; an athletics event may demand precise technique or sustained effort. Explain a specific comparison.
4. Participation, learning and fair play matter even when a child does not win. Competition can be enjoyable, but winning is only one possible result.
5. Practice is something a child can use to improve their present skills; natural ability may affect how easy a task feels at first. The most helpful answer explains why practice and opportunity matter over time.
6. Neither is automatically better. Outdoor activities may offer more space; indoor activities may be easier when weather is poor. The actual facilities, safety and child's preference matter.
7. Both can build fitness when done at a suitable effort and for enough time. The effect depends on how the person swims or runs, how often, and what kind of fitness they mean; swimming also needs suitable supervision.
8. No. Competitive activities can help children practise decisions and fair play, while cooperative or personal-goal activities can also develop skills and enjoyment.
9. This is a personal choice. Structured play gives clear rules and goals; free play gives room to invent. A strong answer names a preference and explains why.
10. Both. Moving uses physical skills, while choosing tactics, concentrating and responding to others use thinking. The balance varies by activity and situation.
11. It depends on the version and task. A passing game needs teammates to coordinate; a relay needs runners to time a handover. Explain what the people need to do together.
12. Reasonable views differ. One argument for grading is that feedback can show progress; one concern is that a single score may overlook effort, improvement and participation. Explain how you would make any assessment fair.

## 8. Real Life Application

1. Where do we use PE skills in daily life?
2. Why is fitness important outside school?
3. How does exercise help mental health?
4. Why do people join sports clubs?
5. What would happen if schools didn’t teach PE?
6. How does sport bring people together?
7. Why do we need physical activity in modern life?
8. How is teamwork used outside sport?
9. Why is fitness important for jobs?
10. What would happen if people stopped exercising?
11. How does sport teach discipline?
12. Should everyone do regular physical activity? Why?

### Suggested answers

1. Running, balance, coordination and carrying or catching appear in play and everyday tasks. For example, keeping balance on a step uses a skill practised in PE.
2. Fitness can help people take part in walking, chores, travel, hobbies and play without tiring as quickly. Different daily tasks call for different qualities.
3. Physical activity can support mood, sleep and a sense of wellbeing, especially when it is enjoyable. It is not a guaranteed cure for a mental health problem.
4. They may want to have fun, learn a skill, meet friends, join a team or compete. People can have more than one reason.
5. Some children might have fewer chances to try a range of movements, games and ways of working with others. Children can be active elsewhere, but school PE offers a shared opportunity.
6. People can enjoy a shared activity, work towards a goal, take turns and encourage one another. Fair play and inclusion help different children feel welcome.
7. Many daily activities involve sitting for long periods. Walking, active play and other suitable movement give people chances to use their bodies regularly.
8. People use teamwork when planning a class project, sharing jobs at home or solving a problem with friends. Listening and offering help transfer beyond sport.
9. Some jobs involve walking, lifting appropriate loads or staying active; others involve long periods of sitting. Fitness can help with daily life around a job too, though job ability depends on many factors.
10. Over time, many people would lose chances to maintain fitness and movement skills. The effect would differ between people; it would not happen all at once after a quiet day.
11. Practising a skill, following agreed rules, being ready for a session and trying again after mistakes can develop self-discipline. A sport does not teach this automatically; the way people take part matters.
12. Regular, suitable physical activity benefits most people, with the type and amount adapted to their abilities and circumstances. For children, active play and everyday movement count; it need not be a formal sport.

## Discussion Prompts

After an answer, ask: **Why do you think that? What might happen if the situation changed? Can you give a real example?** For “which is better?” questions, reward a clear reason and a relevant example rather than insisting on a single choice.
