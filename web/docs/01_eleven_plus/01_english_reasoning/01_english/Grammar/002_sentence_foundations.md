# Sentence Foundations

## What is a sentence?

A sentence expresses a complete thought. Most ordinary sentences have a **subject** and a **predicate**. The subject tells us who or what the sentence is about; the predicate tells us what the subject does or what is true about it.

| Subject | Predicate |
|---|---|
| The cat | slept. |
| The children | went inside. |
| The little bird | sang in the garden. |

A command can also be a complete sentence even when its subject is understood: in “Stop!”, the subject *you* is implied.

## Complete and incomplete sentences

“The cat slept.” is complete: it gives us a whole thought. “Because the cat was tired” is incomplete as a sentence because it leaves us waiting to hear what happened because of the cat's tiredness.

A capital letter and full stop do not make an unfinished thought complete. “After the storm ended.” is incomplete. Add a main idea to finish it: “After the storm ended, we went outside.”

Two complete clauses can also be joined incorrectly: “The sun set the stars appeared.” This is a **run-on sentence**. Join the ideas with a conjunction (“The sun set, and the stars appeared”) or separate them into two sentences (“The sun set. The stars appeared”).

## What is a clause?

A clause is a group of words built around a verb and usually has its own subject. A phrase can add detail without expressing a subject-and-verb idea: *under the old bridge* and *the large brown dog* are phrases; *the dog barked* is a clause.

## Main and subordinate clauses

A **main clause** can stand alone as a complete sentence:

> The children went inside.

A **subordinate clause** depends on a main clause and does not make a complete sentence by itself:

> because it was raining

Join them to make a complete sentence:

> The children went inside because it was raining.

In “Because the path was muddy, the walkers turned back,” *the walkers turned back* is the main clause. *Because the path was muddy* is the subordinate clause; it gives a reason but leaves us asking what happened because the path was muddy.

## How clauses work within sentences

Sentences can contain one clause or connect more than one idea. A subordinate clause adds information to a main clause, such as a reason, time, contrast, or condition:

- **Reason:** We stayed inside because the storm began.
- **Time:** We cheered when the team scored.
- **Contrast:** Although it rained, we played.
- **Condition:** We will play if it stops raining.

If a subordinate clause comes first, a comma normally separates it from the main clause: “When the bell rang, we packed our bags.”

### Try it

1. Is “When the lights went out.” complete or incomplete?
2. In “Although it was cold, we went outside,” which is the main clause?
3. In “The dog barked because it was afraid,” which clause gives the reason?
4. Add a main clause: “After the rain stopped, ...”

### Answers

1. Incomplete; the *when* clause needs a main clause.
2. “We went outside.”
3. “because it was afraid.”
4. Example: “After the rain stopped, we went outside.”
