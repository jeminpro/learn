# FSCE English: Sentence Structure

## 1. Sentences, phrases and clauses

### Start with a sentence

A **sentence** expresses a complete thought. It normally contains a **subject** (who or what the sentence is about) and a **verb** (what happens or what the subject is/does). A sentence begins with a capital letter and ends with suitable punctuation.

> **The curious fox** **waited** beside the gate.

The subject is **the curious fox**; the verb is **waited**. The sentence tells us something complete. In a command such as **Wait here!**, the subject *you* is understood even though it is not written.

**Subject versus predicate:** In *The curious fox waited beside the gate*, the subject is *the curious fox*; the **predicate** is *waited beside the gate*. A useful check is “Who or what?” followed by “What about it?”

### What is a phrase?

A **phrase** is a word or group of words that acts as a unit in a sentence but does not itself make a full statement with its own subject and complete verb.

| Phrase | What it contributes |
|---|---|
| **the curious fox** | Names who is involved: a noun phrase. |
| **beside the gate** | Tells where: a prepositional phrase. |
| **after the storm** | Tells when. |

In school grammar, a noun phrase can be as short as **fox**. The important test is what the words *do* in the whole sentence, not how many words they contain.

### What is a clause?

A **clause** is a group of words built around a verb. In the examples we study here, it has a subject and a verb. Some clauses can stand alone as sentences; others need another clause.

> **The fox waited** **because the gate was closed**.

There are two clauses: *the fox waited* (**fox** + **waited**) and *because the gate was closed* (**gate** + **was**). The second does not complete its meaning on its own because *because* makes us expect more.

### Compare them directly

| Words | What is it? | Why? |
|---|---|---|
| **under the old bridge** | Phrase | No subject and verb making a statement. |
| **the boat passed under the old bridge** | Clause and complete sentence | **boat** is the subject; **passed** is the verb; the thought is complete. |
| **when the boat passed under the old bridge** | Subordinate clause | It has **boat** and **passed**, but *when* leaves us asking what happened then. |

**Try it together:** Turn *under the old bridge* into a sentence. For example: *A heron stood under the old bridge.* Then turn it into a clause that needs a partner: *While a heron stood under the old bridge, ...*

### Complete sentences and fragments

A **fragment** looks like part of a sentence but cannot stand alone in the intended meaning: *Because the path was flooded.* Finish it: *Because the path was flooded, we took another route.* A full stop cannot make an incomplete thought complete.

**Teaching check:** Ask the child to underline the verb, identify who or what it refers to, and then ask whether the words make sense on their own. This is more reliable than counting words.

## 2. Types of clauses

### Main (independent) clauses

A **main clause** makes sense on its own: **The lights went out.** It can be a complete sentence. Two main clauses may be joined: *The lights went out, but the torches still worked.*

### Subordinate (dependent) clauses

A **subordinate clause** adds information to a main clause and cannot normally stand alone in that context. It may tell us **when, why, where, under what condition** or **despite what** something happened.

| Main clause | Added subordinate clause | Complete sentence and meaning |
|---|---|---|
| We stayed indoors. | because the wind was fierce | *We stayed indoors because the wind was fierce.* **Cause** |
| We set off. | when the rain stopped | *We set off when the rain stopped.* **Time** |
| We will play. | if the pitch is dry | *We will play if the pitch is dry.* **Condition** |
| She finished the race. | although she was tired | *Although she was tired, she finished the race.* **Contrast** |

Words such as **because, when, if, although, while, before** can introduce these subordinate clauses. The same idea can often be moved: *When the rain stopped, we set off* / *We set off when the rain stopped*. The first puts the time clue up front. A comma commonly follows an introductory subordinate clause, as in the first version.

### Relative clauses: an explicit FSCE point

A **relative clause** gives information about a noun (or noun phrase). It is a kind of subordinate clause. The guide names **who, which, where, when, whose, that** as common openings.

> The cyclist **who wore a yellow helmet** waved.

The clause *who wore a yellow helmet* tells us **which cyclist**. Its verb is *wore*. It belongs next to *the cyclist*, the person it describes.

| Opening | Example | What the clause explains |
|---|---|---|
| **who** | The girl **who found the map** smiled. | Which girl. |
| **which** | The map, **which was torn**, showed a lake. | More about the map. |
| **that** | The book **that I borrowed** is excellent. | Which book. |
| **whose** | The boy **whose bike broke** walked home. | Which boy; whose bike. |
| **where** | The village **where we stayed** was quiet. | Which village. |
| **when** | The day **when we met** was sunny. | Which day. |

**Meaning matters:** *The pupils who had tickets entered first* identifies a particular group. *The pupils, who had tickets, entered first* presents the ticket information as an aside and suggests all the pupils had tickets. This is a useful reading distinction; detailed comma rules can be studied with punctuation.

**Do not confuse:** *The boy **with a red scarf*** contains a phrase; *The boy **who wore a red scarf*** contains a relative clause, with its own verb *wore*.

### Joining ideas carefully

Choose a joining word for the meaning:

- **because** gives a reason: *We moved inside because it rained.*
- **although** introduces an unexpected contrast: *Although it rained, we played.*
- **if** introduces a condition: *If it rains, we will move inside.*
- **when** gives a time: *When it rained, we moved inside.*

**Try it together:** Ask, “Would changing *because* to *although* change the story?” The child should explain the relationship, not just name the word.

## 3. Types of sentences

There are **two different ways** to classify a sentence: by **how its clauses are built** and by **what the speaker is doing**. Do not mix the two systems.

### By clause structure

| Structure | Rule | Example | Clause count |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | One main clause. It may still have long phrases. | *The tall runner in the blue shirt stumbled.* | One main clause. |
| **Compound** | Two or more main clauses joined appropriately, often by *and, but, or*. | *The runner stumbled, but she recovered.* | Two main clauses. |
| **Complex** | At least one main clause and at least one subordinate clause. | *Although the runner stumbled, she recovered.* | One main plus one subordinate. |

**The length trap:** *The tall runner in the blue shirt with muddy shoes crossed the field* is still simple. Its extra details are phrases, not new clauses. **Count clauses, not words.**

**The “and” trap:** *Ava packed her bag and closed the door* can be treated as one main clause with one subject (*Ava*) and two actions (*packed*, *closed*). *Ava packed her bag, and Ben closed the door* clearly has two main clauses. The word *and* alone does not prove a sentence is compound.

**A run-on sentence** joins complete thoughts without a suitable link or punctuation: *The bell rang we hurried inside.* Repair it: *The bell rang, and we hurried inside.* Or make two sentences. **Comma splice:** *The bell rang, we hurried inside* still needs a suitable join or a stronger boundary.

### By purpose

| Purpose | Example | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Statement** | *The path is narrow.* | Tells us something. |
| **Question** | *Is the path narrow?* | Asks something. |
| **Command** | *Follow the narrow path.* | Tells someone to do something. |
| **Exclamation** | *What a narrow path this is!* | Expresses strong feeling; this form begins with *What* or *How*. |

A sentence may be **complex and a question** at the same time: *Will you wait here while I fetch the map?* “Complex” describes its clauses; “question” describes its purpose. The purpose labels are helpful supporting knowledge; the FSCE parent guide does not list them individually.

### Change the focus without changing the main event

Start: *The children entered the hall.*

- Add a **time phrase**: *After lunch, the children entered the hall.*
- Add a **time clause**: *When the bell rang, the children entered the hall.*
- Add a **relative clause**: *The children who had finished lunch entered the hall.*

The time phrase has no verb; the time clause has *bell rang*; the relative clause describes *the children*. Ask what new information each version supplies.

## 4. Parts of speech

A **part of speech** describes a word's job **in its sentence**. Some words change jobs in different contexts, so read the whole sentence before deciding.

| Word class | Main job | Examples in context |
|---|---|---|
| **Noun** | Names a person, place, thing or idea. | The **artist** showed **courage**. |
| **Pronoun** | Stands in place of, or points to, a noun/noun phrase. | **She** showed **it** to us. |
| **Verb** | Tells an action, event or state. | The artist **painted**; the sky **was** dark. |
| **Adjective** | Describes or gives a quality of a noun. | The **stormy** sky. |
| **Adverb** | Adds information to a verb, adjective, another adverb or a whole clause. | She spoke **quietly**; **Perhaps** it will rain. |
| **Determiner** | Introduces a noun and helps specify which or how many. | **The** boat; **those** boats; **three** boats. |
| **Conjunction** | Joins words, phrases or clauses. | Tea **and** toast; we stayed **because** it rained. |
| **Preposition** | Links a noun phrase to another part of a sentence, often showing time, place or direction. | **Under** the table; **after** lunch. |

**Work through a sentence:** *After lunch, those curious children quietly explored the cave because it was cool.*

- **After** is a preposition introducing *lunch*; **lunch** is a noun.
- **Those** is a determiner; **curious** an adjective; **children** a noun.
- **Quietly** is an adverb; **explored** a verb; **the** a determiner; **cave** a noun.
- **Because** is a conjunction linking the reason; **it** a pronoun; **was** a verb; **cool** an adjective.

### Time, place and cause: an explicit FSCE connection

The guide names **conjunctions, adverbs and prepositions** as ways of connecting ideas of time, place and cause. Compare:

| Form | Example | Clue |
|---|---|---|
| **Conjunction + clause** | *We waited **because the bridge was closed**.* | The words after *because* contain a subject and verb. |
| **Preposition + noun phrase** | *We waited **after lunch**.* | *Lunch* is a noun; there is no new clause. |
| **Adverb** | ***Later**, we crossed the bridge.* | *Later* locates the action in time. |

**A word can change class:** *We left **after lunch*** (*after* + noun phrase: preposition). *We left **after the rain stopped*** (*after* + clause: conjunction). Similarly, *light* is a noun in *the light shone* and an adjective in *a light bag*.

**Pronoun versus determiner:** *This is broken* → **this** works as a pronoun. *This lantern is broken* → **this** introduces the noun *lantern*, so it works as a determiner.

**Avoid a false shortcut:** Not every adverb ends in *-ly* (*soon, here, often*), and not every *-ly* word is an adverb (*friendly* can be an adjective).

## 5. Noun phrases

A **noun phrase** is built around a **head noun** and works as a unit. It may be short (*a lantern*) or expanded (*the old brass lantern beside the door*). Ask: **Which person or thing are these words identifying?**

> **The small, fluffy puppy with a red collar** slept.

The entire bold group is a noun phrase. Its head noun is **puppy**. *The*, *small* and *fluffy* add information before the noun; *with a red collar* adds information after it. This mirrors the kind of expansion explicitly described in the FSCE guide.

### Three useful ways to expand

| Step | Example | Added detail |
|---|---|---|
| Basic noun phrase | **a box** | Names the thing. |
| Add adjective(s) | **a heavy wooden box** | Describes it. |
| Add a noun before the head noun | **a heavy wooden treasure box** | Tells what kind of box. |
| Add a prepositional phrase | **a heavy wooden treasure box beneath the bed** | Identifies it further by place. |

**Head-noun check:** In *a silver bicycle bell*, **bell** is the head noun; *bicycle* specifies what kind of bell. In *the girl with the blue bag*, **girl** is the head of the whole noun phrase; *bag* is the head of a smaller noun phrase inside it.

### Phrase, clause or sentence?

- **The house beside the river**: noun phrase; *beside the river* adds detail with no verb.
- **The house that stood beside the river**: noun phrase containing a **relative clause**; *that stood beside the river* has the verb *stood*.
- **The house beside the river flooded.**: complete sentence with the subject noun phrase *the house beside the river* and verb *flooded*.

### Choose detail that helps the reader

Compare *a thing* → *a box* → *a battered wooden box beneath the stairs*. The last version can help a reader picture and identify the object. More adjectives are not always better: *a huge, enormous, gigantic box* repeats the same idea. Choose details that change what the reader understands.

**Try it together:** Give the child *a bird*. Ask for an expansion with one adjective, then with a prepositional phrase: *a startled bird on the windowsill*. Finally, ask whether *the bird that tapped on the window* has a phrase or a clause after *bird*, and why.

A. The children waited

**3. Which group contains a subject and verb but still needs another clause to complete its thought?**
