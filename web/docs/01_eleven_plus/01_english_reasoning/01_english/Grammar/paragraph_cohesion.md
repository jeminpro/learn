# FSCE English — Sections D & K Teaching Pack

**Paragraphs and cohesion • Linking ideas • Spotting errors • Improving sentences**  
**Parent teaching resource | Up to Year 5 | Original examples and 30-question assessment**

This pack follows **15–17 and 43–44** in your A–L checklist. It builds on your grammar, parts of speech and F–H packs. Your separate punctuation resource can provide detailed teaching of speech marks, apostrophes and commas; this pack includes those errors only in **mixed editing**, where the child must notice what needs fixing.

## How to teach it

Work in short sessions. Start with **meaning**: ask “What is this paragraph about?” and “How does this sentence relate to the previous one?” Then look at the words that make the connection. For editing, ask the child to **read the whole sentence before changing anything**. After a correction, ask for a reason and reread it to ensure the meaning still works.

For a child who struggles with writing speed, begin with choosing between two revisions and explaining the choice aloud. Progress to brief written corrections. A good editor changes **only what needs changing**; a more elaborate sentence is not automatically better.

# D. Paragraphs & Cohesion

## 15. Paragraph organisation

### Why writers use paragraphs

A **paragraph** groups sentences that belong to one main idea or a closely related part of a text. A new paragraph signals that the writer is moving to a different part of the discussion, event or viewpoint. Paragraphing helps a reader follow the text without having to work out every shift independently.

In a story, a paragraph might follow an event, a change in **time or place**, or a new speaker in dialogue. In nonfiction, a paragraph might explain one **aspect of a topic** before moving to another. There is no rule that every paragraph must contain a fixed number of sentences: its **unity and purpose** matter more than its length.

### Find the main idea

Read the whole paragraph, then try to finish: **“This paragraph is mainly about…”** Do not choose a detail that occurs only once if the rest of the paragraph says something broader.

> “Bees carry pollen between flowers as they feed. This helps many plants produce seeds. Some crops depend on pollinators, including bees, to grow fruit.”

Main idea: **Bees help plants reproduce through pollination.** “Bees feed on flowers” is true but too narrow to cover the paragraph. “All insects help crops” is too broad and not established by this paragraph.

### Group related ideas

Imagine arranging information cards before writing:

| Card | Best paragraph |
|---|---|
| The castle walls were built of stone. | What the castle looked like |
| Its towers could be seen from far away. | What the castle looked like |
| The castle was attacked in 1645. | What happened there |
| The defenders ran out of food. | What happened there |

Putting all four sentences into one paragraph is possible, but a break after the description helps readers notice the shift from **appearance** to **history**.

### Recognise a change of topic, time or place

**Topic:** “The garden was full of herbs… [new paragraph] The kitchen had a large fireplace…” The focus moves from garden to kitchen.

**Time:** “All morning, we searched the beach… [new paragraph] By evening, the tide had covered our footprints…” A later phase begins.

**Place:** “Inside the museum, Maya studied a fossil… [new paragraph] Outside, her brother waited beside the fountain…” The scene moves.

**Argument or viewpoint:** “Some people think the new path will make walking safer… [new paragraph] Others worry it will disturb wildlife…” A different position begins.

A paragraph break is not needed whenever one sentence contains a time word. Ask whether the **focus of the writing** has changed enough to help the reader by starting a new group.

### Worked example: where should the break go?

> “Sam examined the old map. He noticed a red cross near the coast. He copied the route into his notebook. That evening, the family gathered around the kitchen table. Sam told them what he had found.”

A sensible break comes before **“That evening…”**. The first three sentences concern Sam's investigation; the last two move to a later scene with his family. The time signal supports the change, but the deeper reason is the new phase of the story.

### Common mistakes

- **One enormous paragraph:** unrelated stages become harder to track.
- **A break after every sentence:** related ideas are needlessly separated.
- **Choosing a tiny detail as the main idea:** “a red cross” may matter, but the first part of the worked example is mainly about **studying the map**.
- **Confusing paragraph order with sentence order:** a paragraph can be well grouped yet still appear in the wrong place in the wider text.

## 16. Cohesion — helping the text hold together

### What cohesion means

A text is **cohesive** when a reader can see how its sentences and paragraphs fit together. A series of individually correct sentences can still be confusing if the links between them are missing or unclear.

Compare:

> “Amina found a stray kitten. Amina took the stray kitten home. The stray kitten drank milk.”

The meaning is understandable but repetitive. A more natural version is:

> “Amina found a stray kitten. **She** took **it** home. **The kitten** drank milk.”

*She* refers to Amina, *it* refers to the kitten, and *the kitten* refreshes the noun so the reader keeps track. Using pronouns **and** repeating a key noun when needed can be clearer than replacing every noun.

### Four ways to build a clear link

| Device | Example | What it tells the reader |
|---|---|---|
| **Pronoun reference** | “Mia found a shell. **She** kept **it**.” | Who and what we are still discussing. |
| **Useful noun repetition or substitution** | “The **volcano** erupted. **The mountain** sent ash into the air.” | Maintains the topic with a clear reference. |
| **Linking word or phrase** | “It began raining. **Therefore**, we went inside.” | How the ideas relate. |
| **Logical order and consistent time** | “First she mixed the batter. Later she baked it.” | The sequence of events. |

### Pronouns must point clearly

“Lucy told Sara **she** had lost the key.” Who lost it? It might be Lucy or Sara. The pronoun *she* is **ambiguous**. Rewrite according to the intended meaning:

- “Lucy told Sara, ‘**I** have lost the key.’” Lucy lost it.
- “Lucy told Sara that **Sara** had lost the key.” Sara lost it.

Do not avoid every pronoun; instead, replace a pronoun with a noun when the reader might reasonably lose track. “The dog chased the cat. **It** ran into the garden” is ambiguous because either animal might have run.

### Nouns can prevent too many vague *it*s and *they*s

> “The children found a wooden box beside a suitcase. They opened it.”

Who does *they* refer to? Likely the children. What does *it* refer to? The box or suitcase? Improve the second sentence: “The children opened **the wooden box**.” A precise noun resolves the uncertainty.

Sometimes a related noun carries the idea forward: “The storm arrived at dawn. **The heavy rain** flooded the road.” The second sentence tells us what part of the storm caused the flooding.

### Link sentences *and* paragraphs

Within a paragraph: “The path was icy. **As a result**, we walked slowly.” The link shows cause and effect.

Across paragraphs:

> “The old bridge was too narrow for modern buses. Engineers proposed a new crossing.  
>  
> **However,** local residents worried that the crossing would affect the park.”

The new paragraph introduces an opposing concern. *However* helps, but the **meaning of the two paragraphs** must actually contrast. A linking word cannot fix an illogical relationship.

### Order information so it can be followed

For instructions, order often follows time: “First, wash the fruit. Next, cut it. Finally, serve it.” For an explanation, order may follow cause and result: “Rain soaked the ground. As a result, the match was delayed.” For a comparison, describe one aspect in each place or give one viewpoint and then the other. The “correct” order depends on the text's purpose.

**Worked revision:** “The door opened. It was in a box. They ran away.” The references are unclear. One possible clear story is: “**The children found a box. When they opened it, a mouse jumped out. The children ran away.**” Notice that this revision adds missing information to make the sequence understandable; it is not the only possible story.

## 17. Linking ideas — choosing a relationship

Linking words are useful when they express a **real relationship** between ideas. Your checklist groups them by **time**, **place**, **number/order**, and **cause/effect**. Some also signal contrast or an example. The same idea can sometimes be expressed by a conjunction in one sentence or a linking adverb at the start of another; read the surrounding grammar.

### Time and sequence

| Word | Example | What it suggests |
|---|---|---|
| **first** | **First**, we planned the route. | Beginning of a sequence. |
| **next** | **Next**, we checked the map. | Following step. |
| **then** | We **then** set off. | Following event. |
| **afterwards** | We stopped. **Afterwards**, we ate. | Later, after the previous event. |
| **finally** | **Finally**, we reached the hill. | Last step or outcome. |

These words do not supply the entire order for us. “Finally, we packed our bags. First, we returned home” is odd if packing was meant to occur before returning home. Check the actual sequence.

### Place and movement through a description

**Nearby, above, beyond** help a reader locate things. “A lighthouse stood on the cliff. **Below**, waves struck the rocks. **Beyond** the harbour, we saw an island.” *Beyond* gives a more distant position or location. These words may work as adverbs or prepositions depending on their use: “The village lies **nearby**” versus “The village lies **beyond the hill**.” For this lesson, prioritise the **spatial relationship** each one conveys.

### Number and order in explanations

**Firstly, secondly, finally** can signpost distinct points: “**Firstly**, the path is safer. **Secondly**, it is shorter. **Finally**, it avoids the steep hill.” Do not add these mechanically to every story. They fit an explanation or argument where the reader benefits from counting reasons or steps. **First** and **second** can also do the job more simply.

### Cause, result and contrast

| Link | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **because** | We stayed in **because** it rained. | Gives a reason within the sentence. |
| **therefore** | It rained. **Therefore**, we stayed in. | Presents the second idea as a result. |
| **as a result** | It rained. **As a result**, the ground flooded. | Explicit consequence. |
| **however** | It rained. **However**, we still played. | Contrast (useful additional connector). |

“It rained. **Therefore**, I enjoyed the music” does not make a logical link unless the text supplies a reason why rain caused that enjoyment. “It rained. **However**, we played outside” does show a plausible contrast. A reader must check the *ideas*, not select a connector by memorising a list.

**Because** normally introduces a clause: “because **it rained**.” To link to a noun phrase, use “**because of the rain**.” “Therefore” often links a new sentence or clause to an earlier conclusion; it does not replace *because* word for word in “We stayed in therefore it rained.”

### Worked choice

> “The first train was cancelled. ___, we caught a later train.”

**Therefore** or **As a result** makes sense: cancellation led to the later train. **Meanwhile** would mean something was happening at the same time, so it would misrepresent this sequence. **However** would suggest an unexpected contrast that this sentence does not establish.

### Practice 17

**17A.** “We washed the apples. ___, we sliced them.” Choose *Next* or *However*.  
**17B.** “The road was icy. ___, the trip was delayed.” Choose *Therefore* or *Nearby*.  
**17C.** “The tree stood by the pond. The path ran ___ the pond.” Choose *beyond* or *secondly* if the path continued farther than the pond.  
**17D.** “We expected a long wait. ___, the bus arrived immediately.” Choose *However* or *As a result*.  
**17E.** “The roof leaked. We moved the books.” Join the two ideas with *because* while preserving the cause.

# K. Editing & Language Accuracy

## 43. Spotting errors

Editing means checking whether the words **fit the intended meaning** and whether the sentence follows standard written English. A word may be correctly spelt but wrong **in context**: *their* is a real word, yet “Their going home” needs **They're**. Not every unusual sentence contains an error; understand it before changing it.

### A useful editing order

1. **Read the entire sentence** and say what it means.
2. **Find the subject and verb.** Check whether they agree and the time is clear.
3. **Track references.** Does each pronoun clearly point to the intended person or thing?
4. **Check words that sound alike** and the intended meaning.
5. **Read punctuation aloud.** Does it separate or clarify ideas as intended?
6. **Reread the corrected sentence.** A correction should solve the original problem without creating a new one.

### Error types from your checklist

| Error type | Unedited example | Better version | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tense** | Yesterday she **walks** home. | Yesterday she **walked** home. | The action happened in the past. |
| **Progressive form** | They **was playing**. | They **were playing**. | *They* takes *were* in standard English. |
| **Subject–verb agreement** | The box of pencils **are** heavy. | The box of pencils **is** heavy. | *Box* is the singular subject. |
| **Pronoun reference** | Mia told Zoe **she** won. | Mia told Zoe that **Mia** won. | Names the intended winner. |
| **Apostrophe** | The **girls** coat is red. | The **girl's** coat is red. | One girl owns the coat. |
| **Comma** | **After lunch we** left. | **After lunch, we** left. | Marks the opening time phrase. |
| **Speech punctuation** | Tom said I am ready. | Tom said, **“I am ready.”** | Separates Tom's exact words. |
| **Homophone** | Put it over **their**. | Put it over **there**. | *There* indicates a place. |
| **Spelling** | We **recieved** the letter. | We **received** the letter. | Correct spelling is *received*. |
| **Word choice** | He **borrowed** me a pencil. | He **lent** me a pencil. | The giver lends; the recipient borrows. |
| **Sentence structure** | Because it was late. | **We went home because it was late.** | The first version needs a main clause. |

The punctuation examples are **quick editing checks**, not full lessons. Refer to your punctuation resource for why and when to use each mark, including the difference between **girl's** and **girls'**.

### Multiple errors: fix them in a sensible order

> “Yesterday, the childrens was running through the park and see a fox.”

First decide that **children** is a plural noun (not *childrens*); then **were running** matches it; then **saw** fits yesterday's past time. A clear version is:

> “Yesterday, the **children were running** through the park and **saw** a fox.”

Notice that a progressive action (*were running*) and simple past event (*saw*) can **correctly coexist**: they express different ways of viewing the actions. Do not change every verb to the same form blindly.

### A good editor does not invent mistakes

“Yesterday I was tired, but today I feel better” changes tense **correctly** because time changes. “The dog barked and jumped” is a complete sentence even though there is no second written subject. In mixed exercises, some options may already be correct; check the meaning before “fixing” them.

## 44. Sentence improvement

A sentence can be **grammatically correct** but unclear, repetitive, vague or poorly connected to its neighbours. Improving it means making the reader's job easier while keeping the intended meaning. Use this sequence:

1. **Keep the meaning:** What exactly is the writer trying to say?
2. **Find the problem:** Unclear reference? Repeated nouns? Vague verb? Missing relationship? Too much or too little detail?
3. **Make one focused change:** Do not decorate every word.
4. **Check the result:** Is it more accurate and natural for the intended reader?

### Improve clarity

“Ben saw Tom when **he** entered the room” is unclear because *he* could refer to Ben or Tom. If Tom entered: “Ben saw Tom **enter the room**.” If Ben entered: “**When Ben entered the room**, he saw Tom.” A revision must reflect the intended event; there is no single correct version without that context.

### Remove needless repetition without losing the subject

Before: “The girl opened the book. The girl read the book. The girl closed the book.”  
After: “The girl opened the book, read **it**, and then closed **it**.”

But repeating a noun is sometimes clearer: “Maya showed Leila the book. **Leila** thanked her.” Replacing *Leila* with *she* could make the reader unsure who thanked whom.

### Choose precise vocabulary

Before: “The animal **went** across the garden.”  
After: “The rabbit **darted** across the garden.”

*Darted* suggests quick, sudden movement. It should be used only if that is what happened. “He was **nice**” could become “He **offered to help carry the bags**” if the action is known; a concrete detail can be more informative than a vague evaluation.

### Add appropriate detail

“A bag lay on the floor” is already clear. If identifying the bag matters, “**A torn canvas bag** lay **beside the back door**” gives helpful description and position. Extra detail is not always helpful: “a bag that was really very extremely torn and rather old” repeats the idea and slows the sentence.

### Improve sentence structure

Before: “Because the path was blocked.” This is incomplete.  
After: “**We turned back because the path was blocked.**”

Before: “The rain stopped the children went out.” Two main clauses are pressed together.  
After: “**The rain stopped, and the children went out.**” Or “**When the rain stopped, the children went out.**” The second revision makes the time relationship explicit.

### Connect ideas logically

Before: “The floor was wet. **However**, we wiped it.” *However* signals contrast, but wiping is a plausible **response** to the wet floor.  
After: “The floor was wet, **so** we wiped it.”

Before: “The floor was wet. **However**, no one had used water.” Now *however* expresses a genuine contrast or surprise.

### Different audiences may need different revisions

For a young child, “The canine advanced rapidly towards the exit” may be less helpful than “The dog ran to the door.” For an informative report, “The doggie zoomed away” might be too informal. A good improvement serves **audience and purpose**, which you cover in section L.

### Worked comparison: choose the best revision

Original: “The boy saw a thing in the garden. It was good.”  
A. “The boy saw a thing in the garden, and it was nice and good and wonderful.”  
B. “The boy spotted a hedgehog beneath the hedge and watched it curl into a ball.”

If the actual thing was a hedgehog and this is what happened, **B** gives precise information and a clear image. If those facts are unknown, **do not invent them**: ask what the writer meant or revise only what the available facts allow. Accuracy comes before vividness.

### Practice 44

**44C.** Make the relationship logical: “The train was delayed. However, we arrived late.”

**Answers 44:** A. “Mina picked up the map and folded **it**.” B. “The dog chased the cat, and **the cat** escaped.” C. “The train was delayed. **As a result,** we arrived late.” D.
