# Verbs, Tenses and Subject–Verb Agreement

A **verb** tells us about an action (*jump, whispered*), an occurrence (*arrived, happened*) or a state (*is, seems, became*). Verbs can change form to show time and to agree with their subject.

## Tense and time

English verbs can refer to the **present**, **past** or **future**. The verb form and the sentence context work together to show when something happens. Time words such as *yesterday, now, next week,* and *every morning* are clues, but the verb still needs to fit the time.

| Time | Example | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Present | Mia **walks** to school. | Habit, general truth, or present state. |
| Past | Mia **walked** to school yesterday. | An event or state in the past. |
| Future | Mia **will walk** to school tomorrow. | An event expected or planned for the future. |

The simple present often describes a habit or general truth rather than an action happening at this exact second: “I **brush** my teeth every morning”; “Water **freezes** when it is cold enough”; “She **knows** the answer.”

## Simple forms

### Simple present

Use the base verb with **I, you, we,** and **they**; use the verb plus **-s** or **-es** with **he, she,** and **it**:

- I **walk** to school. / She **walks** to school.
- The sun **rises** in the east.
- Trains **leave** at six o'clock.

### Simple past

The simple past describes an event or state that happened earlier. Many verbs add **-ed** (*play → played; jump → jumped*). Some spellings change (*live → lived; try → tried; stop → stopped*). Other verbs are irregular:

| Present | Past | Present | Past |
|---|---|---|---|
| go | went | come | came |
| see | saw | say | said |
| eat | ate | give | gave |
| run | ran | take | took |
| write | wrote | think | thought |
| have | had | do | did |

Examples: “He **played** football yesterday”; “They **went** to Dubai last year.”

### Simple future

Use **will + base verb** to describe an event expected in the future: “I **will visit** my friend”; “They **will study** later.”

## Progressive forms

A **progressive** form presents an action as in progress at a particular time. It uses a form of **be** plus a verb ending in **-ing**.

| Form | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Present progressive | am/is/are + verb-ing | She **is reading**. |
| Past progressive | was/were + verb-ing | He **was cooking** when the phone rang. |
| Future progressive | will be + verb-ing | They **will be studying** at seven. |

The whole verb form includes both parts: in “The players **were running**,” the progressive form is **were running**, not just *running*. An *-ing* word by itself does not make a progressive verb: “The boy **running** down the road waved” has *waved* as its main verb.

Compare “Sasha **reads** after dinner” (a habit) with “Sasha **is reading** after dinner” (an action in progress). In “I **was cooking** when the doorbell rang,” the cooking was already in progress when the shorter event happened. Progressive forms do not, by themselves, say whether the action was eventually completed.

The helping verb must match the subject: “She **is reading**”; “They **are reading**”; “He **was reading**”; “We **were reading**.” Use *making* (drop final *e*), *running* (double final consonant), and *lying* (change *ie* to *y*).

## Perfect forms

A **perfect** form connects an earlier event or state to a later reference time. It uses a form of **have** plus a **past participle** (for example, *finished, eaten, gone*).

| Form | Pattern | Example | Typical relationship |
|---|---|---|---|
| Present perfect | have/has + past participle | She **has finished** her homework. | Earlier than now, with a connection to the present. |
| Past perfect | had + past participle | They **had left** when I arrived. | Completed before another past event. |
| Future perfect | will have + past participle | She **will have finished** by five. | Expected to be complete before a future time. |

Present perfect can describe experience (“I **have visited** London”) or a situation that began earlier and continues (“I **have lived** here for five years”). The surrounding words clarify which meaning is intended.

## Perfect progressive forms

Perfect progressive forms focus on an action continuing over a period up to a reference time:

| Form | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Present perfect progressive | have/has been + verb-ing | She **has been studying** for two hours. |
| Past perfect progressive | had been + verb-ing | They **had been waiting** for an hour when the bus came. |
| Future perfect progressive | will have been + verb-ing | By June, I **will have been working** here for ten years. |

These longer forms are useful to recognise; focus on the pattern and the time relationship rather than memorising a label without understanding the sentence.

## Quick reference: common tense and aspect patterns

These combinations are sometimes taught as twelve tense forms: three time references paired with simple, progressive, perfect and perfect progressive patterns.

| Time and form | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Simple present | run/runs | She **runs**. |
| Simple past | past form | She **ran**. |
| Simple future | will + base verb | She **will run**. |
| Present progressive | am/is/are + verb-ing | She **is running**. |
| Past progressive | was/were + verb-ing | She **was running**. |
| Future progressive | will be + verb-ing | She **will be running**. |
| Present perfect | have/has + past participle | She **has run**. |
| Past perfect | had + past participle | She **had run**. |
| Future perfect | will have + past participle | She **will have run**. |
| Present perfect progressive | have/has been + verb-ing | She **has been running**. |
| Past perfect progressive | had been + verb-ing | She **had been running**. |
| Future perfect progressive | will have been + verb-ing | She **will have been running**. |

## Keep the time line consistent

Connected events happening in the same past time frame normally use past forms: “Ella **opened** the box, **found** a note and **read** it.” “Ella **opened** the box, **finds** a note and **read** it” changes tense without a reason.

A tense change is correct when the time changes: “Last year I **walked** to school; now I **cycle**.” Quoted speech can also use a different tense from its reporting verb: “Mia **said**, ‘I **am** ready.’” Do not force every verb in a sentence into one tense; check what each verb refers to.

## Direct and reported speech

**Direct speech** gives a speaker's exact words, usually inside quotation marks: “Ben said, ‘I am tired.’” **Reported speech** gives the meaning without quoting the exact words: “Ben said that he was tired.” The verb that introduces the speech (*said, told, asked,* or *replied*) is the **reporting verb**.

When the reporting verb is in the past, reported speech often changes the original tense. This is called **backshift**. Pronouns and time or place words may also need to change so they fit the new speaker and context.

| Direct speech | Common reported form |
|---|---|
| “I **am** happy.” | He said he **was** happy. |
| “We **are** late.” | They said they **were** late. |
| “I **have finished**.” | She said she **had finished**. |
| “I **will go** tomorrow.” | He said he **would go** the next day. |
| “I **can help**.” | She said she **could help**. |
| “I **may come**.” | He said he **might come**. |
| “I **have been studying**.” | He said he **had been studying**. |

Pronouns change to match who is speaking and who is being spoken to: “I love **my** cat,” said Emma. → Emma said **she** loved **her** cat. Time and place words can shift too, depending on when and where the report is made: *today → that day; tomorrow → the next day; now → then; here → there*.

Reported questions use **asked if/whether** for a yes/no question, followed by statement word order: “Are you coming?” asked John. → John asked **if I was coming**. Do not keep the question's subject–verb inversion (*if was I coming*). The reported question normally ends with a full stop, not a question mark.

Backshift is common, not automatic. If the reporting verb is present, the tense usually stays the same: “He says, ‘I am hungry.’” → He says he **is** hungry. A speaker may also leave a statement in the present when it remains true: “The teacher said that water **boils** at 100°C.” Choose a tense that fits the reporting context and intended meaning.

## Subject–verb agreement

The **subject** tells us who or what the sentence is about. The verb must agree with it in number. Find the head noun of a longer subject and do not let nearby words distract you.

| Subject | Verb | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Singular | Often verb + **-s** in the simple present | The dog **barks**. She **likes** ice cream. |
| Plural | Usually base verb in the simple present | The dogs **bark**. They **like** ice cream. |

The ending changes direction between nouns and verbs: plural nouns often end in *-s* (*bird → birds*), but a third-person singular present verb often ends in *-s* (*one bird sings; two birds sing*). The past form *played* stays the same with singular and plural subjects.

### Find the real subject

Ask “Who or what is doing this?” or “Who or what is being described?” In “The necklace with three bright beads **is** mine,” the head noun is *necklace*, so the subject is singular. In “The beads on the necklace **are** bright,” the subject is *beads*, so it is plural. Ignore prepositional phrases and other extra detail when choosing the verb:

- The **bag** of apples **is** heavy.
- The **apples** in the bag **are** fresh.
- A **friend** of my parents **lives** nearby.
- My **friends** from school **live** nearby.

### Common agreement patterns

- **Be, have, do:** “He **is** ready”; “They **are** ready.” “The child **has** a pencil”; “The children **have** pencils.” “She **does** the work”; “They **do** the work.”
- **Progressive forms:** The helping verb agrees: “The child **is playing**”; “The children **are playing**.”
- **There is / there are:** Look at the noun that follows: “There **is** one book”; “There **are** three books.”
- **Indefinite pronouns:** *Everyone, somebody, nobody,* and *each* are usually singular: “Everyone **is** happy”; “Nobody **likes** rain.”
- **Either/neither:** *Either* and *neither* are generally treated as singular when used alone: “Neither of the answers **is** correct”; “Either plan **works**.”
- **Subjects joined by and:** Two subjects joined by *and* usually take a plural verb: “Tom and Jerry **are** friends.” When the words name one combined thing or idea, a singular verb may be used: “Bread and butter **is** my favourite.”
- **Subjects joined by or/nor:** The verb usually agrees with the nearer subject: “Either the teacher or the students **are** coming”; “Either the students or the teacher **is** coming.”

In British English, some collective nouns such as *team* can take a singular or plural verb depending on whether the group is viewed as one unit or as its members. Use clear singular and plural subjects when first practising the rule.

### Agreement check

1. Find the main verb.
2. Find the subject and its head noun.
3. Ignore extra words between the subject and verb.
4. Check whether the subject is singular or plural and choose the matching verb.

**Worked example:** “The keys in the drawer (is / are) missing.” What is missing? **The keys**. *Keys* is plural, so choose **are**.
