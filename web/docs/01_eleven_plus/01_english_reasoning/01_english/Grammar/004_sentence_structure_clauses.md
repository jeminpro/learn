# Sentence Structure and Clauses

Sentences can contain one clause or connect several related ideas. Recognising the clauses and the words that join them helps you explain how a sentence is built and what it means.

## What is a clause?

A **clause** is a group of words built around a verb and usually containing its own subject. A **main clause** can stand alone as a complete sentence: “The children went inside.” A **subordinate clause** depends on another clause and does not usually make a complete sentence by itself: “because it was raining.”

Together, they make a complete sentence: “The children went inside because it was raining.” The main clause gives the main idea; the subordinate clause adds a reason.

## Simple, compound and complex sentences

| Sentence type | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | One main clause | The dog barked. |
| **Compound** | Two or more main clauses joined together | The dog barked, and the cat hid. |
| **Complex** | A main clause and at least one subordinate clause | The cat hid because the dog barked. |

A simple sentence is not necessarily short: “The very small, unusually noisy dog barked loudly at the gate” has one main clause. Nor does every *and* create a compound sentence: “The dog barked and jumped” has one subject doing two actions. “The dog barked, and the cat jumped” has two subjects and two main clauses.

### Identify the sentence pattern

1. Find the verbs and the subject linked to each verb.
2. Decide how many clause-level ideas there are. A shared subject can have two actions in one clause.
3. Test whether each clause can stand alone as written. Two independent clauses can form a compound sentence. A clause introduced by a subordinating conjunction usually depends on another clause, making the sentence complex.

| Sentence | Why? | Type |
|---|---|---|
| The dog and the cat slept. | One main clause with a joined subject. | Simple |
| The dog barked and scratched the door. | One subject with two actions. | Simple |
| The dog barked, and the cat hid. | Two main clauses. | Compound |
| The cat hid because the dog barked. | A main clause plus a dependent reason clause. | Complex |

## Coordinating conjunctions

**Coordinating conjunctions** join words, phrases or clauses of equal importance. Common examples include **and, but, or, so, yet, nor**.

- **And** adds an idea: “The bell rang, and the children left.”
- **But** shows contrast: “The runner stumbled, but her coach caught her.”
- **Or** offers a choice: “We can read or play chess.”
- **So** can show a result: “The hall was closing, so we left.”

When a coordinating conjunction joins two main clauses, each part can stand alone: “The bell rang. The children left.” A comma is normally used before the conjunction joining two complete clauses. Do not rely only on spotting *and* or *but*; check whether each side has its own subject-and-verb idea.

## Subordinating conjunctions

A **subordinating conjunction** introduces a subordinate clause and signals how it relates to the main clause.

| Conjunction | Relationship | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **because** | Reason | We stayed inside because the storm began. |
| **when** | Time | We cheered when the team scored. |
| **while** | Two actions at the same time | I read while my sister drew. |
| **although** | Contrast | Although it rained, we played. |
| **if** | Condition | We will play if it stops raining. |
| **unless** | Condition meaning “if not” | We will be late unless we leave now. |

Choosing the right word is a meaning decision as well as a grammar decision. “She carried an umbrella **because** it was raining” gives a reason. “She carried an umbrella **although** it was sunny” presents a contrast.

## Joining clauses

Clauses can be joined in different ways to make compound and complex sentences:

- Join two main clauses with a coordinating conjunction: “The sun set, and the stars appeared.”
- Join a main clause to a subordinate reason clause: “We stayed inside because the storm began.”
- Put the subordinate clause first: “Because the storm began, we stayed inside.”

When a subordinate clause begins the sentence, a comma normally separates it from the main clause. Compare “When the alarm sounded, everyone left” with “Everyone left when the alarm sounded.” Both show the same time relationship, but the first brings the time detail forward.

Avoid **sentence fragments** and **run-ons**. “Because the path was muddy.” is a fragment because the subordinate clause leaves us waiting for the main idea. “The sun set the stars appeared” is a run-on because two main clauses have been pressed together without a suitable join. Repair it as “The sun set, and the stars appeared” or “The sun set. The stars appeared.”

A **comma splice** is a run-on in which two complete clauses are joined by only a comma: “I was tired, I went to bed.” A comma by itself is not enough. Repair it with a coordinating conjunction (“I was tired, **so** I went to bed”), a full stop (“I was tired. I went to bed”), or a semicolon when the clauses are closely related (“I was tired; I went to bed”).

## Combining and transforming sentences

**Sentence combination** joins two or more short sentences into one smoother sentence. Choose a joining method that preserves the intended relationship:

- Use a **coordinating conjunction** to join equal ideas: “I wanted to go out. It was raining.” → “I wanted to go out, **but** it was raining.”
- Use a **subordinating conjunction** when one idea depends on another: “She was tired. She stayed up late.” → “She stayed up late **although** she was tired.”
- Use a **relative clause** to attach information to a noun: “I met a girl. She plays the piano.” → “I met a girl **who plays the piano**.”
- Use a **semicolon** between closely related main clauses: “The bus was late; we missed school.”

Check that the connector expresses the intended meaning. For example, *because* gives a reason, *although* signals contrast, and *so* introduces a result. A sentence can be grammatically joined but illogical if the ideas do not fit: “He ate dinner because it was red.”

**Sentence transformation** changes the form or structure of a sentence. Preserve its core idea where possible, while recognising that changing a statement into a command or question also changes its purpose.

| Sentence type | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Statement** | Tells or reports something | The train is late. |
| **Question** | Asks something | Is the train late? |
| **Command (imperative)** | Tells someone to do something; the subject *you* is usually understood | Close the window. |
| **Exclamation** | Expresses strong feeling | What a fast train! |

To form a question, place a helping verb before the subject when one is available: “You **are** ready.” → “**Are** you ready?” With an ordinary present or past verb, use **do, does,** or **did**: “You like tea.” → “Do you like tea?” / “She likes tea.” → “Does she like tea?”

Commands usually begin with the base verb: “You must open the door.” → “Open the door!” Exclamations commonly use **What** or **How**: “It is a beautiful day.” → “What a beautiful day!” / “How beautiful the day is!” Choose a form that sounds natural and expresses the intended feeling.

## Identifying main and subordinate clauses

Use this method:

1. Find the verb in each clause.
2. Identify the subject connected to each verb.
3. Check whether each part can stand alone as written. The one that can is a main clause; the dependent part is subordinate.
4. Ask what the subordinate clause contributes: reason, time, contrast, condition or another detail.

**Worked example:** “Because the path was muddy, the walkers turned back.” The verb groups are *was* and *turned*. “The walkers turned back” can stand alone, so it is the main clause. “Because the path was muddy” depends on it and gives the reason, so it is subordinate.

## Relative clauses

A **relative clause** is a type of subordinate clause that adds information about a **noun**. The noun it describes is called its **antecedent**.

> The boy **who won the race** is my friend.

The relative clause **who won the race** describes the noun **boy**. It tells us which boy. The main clause is “The boy is my friend.”

### Relative pronouns and relative words

| Relative word | Common use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **who** | A person | The girl **who found the key** smiled. |
| **which** | A thing or animal | The book **which has a red cover** is mine. |
| **that** | A person or thing in many identifying clauses | The coat **that I bought** is warm. |
| **whose** | Possession or association | The boy **whose bag was lost** asked for help. |
| **where** | A place | This is the park **where we met**. |
| **when** | A time | I remember the day **when we moved**. |

*Whose* means belonging to whom; it is not the same as *who's* (“who is” or “who has”). In identifying clauses, *that* can often be used instead of *who* or *which*: “the child who won” / “the child that won”; “the book which I borrowed” / “the book that I borrowed.”

### What relative clauses do

A relative clause attaches extra information to a noun, helping identify it or telling us more about it. One way to build one is to combine two sentences about the same person or thing:

> I met a scientist. The scientist studies volcanoes.
>
> I met a scientist **who studies volcanoes**.

The clause **who studies volcanoes** describes **scientist**, not *I* or the action *met*. Ask: **Which noun does the clause describe? What information does it add about that noun?**

### Identifying information and extra information

“The **book that has a red cover** is mine” uses the clause to identify which book. This information is needed to identify the noun and normally has no commas around it.

“My **bike, which has a red frame,** is outside” adds information about a bike that is already identified. The commas set off the extra information. Do not put commas around every relative clause automatically. In these examples, *that* is used for identifying information; *which* introduces the extra, comma-enclosed information.

### Relative clauses or other subordinate clauses?

Words such as *when* and *where* can start different kinds of clauses. Check whether the clause describes a noun or tells us about the action in the main clause.

| Example | What the clause does | Type |
|---|---|---|
| I remember **the day when we moved**. | Describes the noun *day*. | Relative clause |
| **When we moved**, I was excited. | Tells when I was excited. | Time clause |
| This is **the park where we met**. | Describes the noun *park*. | Relative clause |
| I smiled **because we met**. | Gives a reason for smiling. | Reason clause |

### Common errors

- “The child **whose** coat was lost” is correct; “the child **who's** coat was lost” is not.
- A relative clause describes a noun: in “The song **that we heard** was beautiful,” the clause describes *song*.
- Some identifying relative clauses can omit the relative pronoun when it is the object: “The cake **Mum baked**” means “The cake **that Mum baked**.” Do not omit it when it is the subject: “The cake **that fell**” cannot become “The cake fell” with the same meaning and structure.
- Do not confuse “the place **where we met**” (relative clause describing *place*) with “**When we met**, I was happy” (time clause describing when I was happy).

4. “We went outside.”

6. *because*.
7. Example: “Although the puzzle was difficult, we solved it.”
