# Parts of Speech

Every word has a job in a sentence. That job is its **part of speech**. Knowing the job helps you understand sentences and choose or correct words in grammar questions. A word's job can change with its use, so always look at the whole sentence.

## Quick reference

| Part of speech | Main job | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun | Names a person, place, thing or idea | The **crowd** cheered. |
| Verb | Shows an action, occurrence or state | The crowd **cheered**. |
| Adjective | Describes a noun or pronoun | The **excited** crowd cheered. |
| Adverb | Adds information about a verb, adjective or adverb | The crowd cheered **loudly**. |
| Pronoun | Refers to a noun or noun phrase | **They** cheered. |
| Preposition | Shows a relationship, often in a prepositional phrase | They cheered **after the match**. |
| Conjunction | Joins words, phrases or clauses | They cheered **because** they had won. |
| Determiner | Specifies a noun: which one, whose, or how many | **The** crowd cheered. |
| Interjection | Expresses a reaction or feeling | **Hurray!** They had won. |

Articles (*a, an, the*) are a type of determiner. Some traditional school materials count articles separately; the important point is to identify the word's job in its sentence.

## 1. Nouns: naming words

A **noun** names a person, place, thing or idea. It can be the subject of a sentence or appear elsewhere in it.

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Common | General name | city, teacher, river |
| Proper | Specific name, usually capitalised | London, Aisha, River Severn |
| Collective | A group named as one unit | team, choir, flock |
| Abstract | An idea, quality, feeling or state | courage, kindness, fear |

In “The team of players arrived,” *team* is the collective noun; *players* is an ordinary plural noun. A proper noun names a specific person or place: *city* is common, while *Gloucester* is proper. Abstract nouns name things we can discuss but not usually hold, such as *hope* or *pride*.

Words can do different jobs in different sentences: “A **run** around the park was fun” uses *run* as a noun; “I **run** around the park” uses it as a verb. Decide from the sentence, not from the word alone.

**Try it:** In “Maya showed great patience,” identify the proper noun and abstract noun.

## 2. Verbs: actions, occurrences and states

A **verb** tells us about an action, occurrence or state. In “The fox **jumped**,” it shows an action. In “The fox **is** hungry,” *is* links the subject to a description.

### Action and linking verbs

Action verbs include visible actions (*run, lift, build*) and less visible ones (*think, remember*). A **linking verb** connects the subject to a description or identity: “The soup **smells** delicious”; “He **became** a doctor.” Forms of *be* (*am, is, are, was, were*) often link a subject to a description.

The same verb can be an action or a linking verb, depending on context:

| Sentence | Job of the verb |
|---|---|
| The cook **tasted** the soup. | Action: the cook did the tasting. |
| The soup **tasted** salty. | Linking: *tasted* links soup to *salty*. |
| She **looked** at the clock. | Action: she used her eyes. |
| She **looked** tired. | Linking: *looked* links her to *tired*. |

### Verb groups

A verb can contain more than one word. In “They **were running**,” *were* is a helping verb and *running* is the main verb. Other examples include *has finished* and *might arrive*. When asked for the whole verb group, include the helping verb.

**Try it:** Is *felt* an action or linking verb in “I felt the rough stone” and “The stone felt rough”? Find the whole verb group in “The children were laughing.”

## 3. Adjectives: describing and comparing

An **adjective** gives information about a noun or pronoun: “a **heavy** bag”; “The bag is **heavy**.” Adjectives can describe size, colour, shape, age or another quality. They often come before a noun, but can also follow a linking verb.

Adjectives can compare:

| Basic | Comparative: compare two | Superlative: highest in a group |
|---|---|---|
| tall | taller | tallest |
| nice | nicer | nicest |
| happy | happier | happiest |
| big | bigger | biggest |
| careful | more careful | most careful |
| good | better | best |
| bad | worse | worst |

Avoid doubling the comparison: use **taller**, not *more taller*; use **tallest**, not *most tallest*. Some adjectives allow more than one natural form, such as *cleverer* and *more clever*.

An adjective describes a noun; an adverb can describe an action: “The **quick** runner moved **quickly**.” With a linking verb, use the adjective: “The soup smells **wonderful**.”

**Try it:** Identify the adjectives in “The tiny silver key opened the door.” Complete: “Of the three roads, this is the (wide) ______.”

## 4. Adverbs: adding detail

An **adverb** can add information about a verb, adjective or another adverb. It can tell how, where, when or how often something happens, or show degree.

| Kind | Question | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Manner | How? | quietly, carefully, well |
| Place | Where? | here, outside, nearby |
| Time | When? | now, yesterday, soon |
| Frequency | How often? | always, often, sometimes, never |
| Degree | To what extent? | very, quite |

Many manner adverbs end in *-ly*, but not all adverbs do: *often, here,* and *soon* are adverbs too. Also, not every *-ly* word is an adverb: *friendly* is an adjective in “a friendly neighbour.” Ask what the word adds rather than relying on its ending.

Some words can have different jobs. “The children waited **outside**” uses *outside* as an adverb. “They waited **outside the shop**” uses it as a preposition. In “The water was **very** cold,” *very* modifies the adjective *cold*.

**Try it:** Classify the adverbs in “Yesterday, Aisha carefully placed the box here.”

## 5. Pronouns: referring without repetition

A **pronoun** refers to a noun or noun phrase: “**Lucy** found a key. **She** kept **it** safe.” *She* refers to Lucy; *it* refers to the key. The person or thing a pronoun refers to is its **referent**. Make sure the reader can tell what that referent is.

| Job | Examples | Example sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Subject: does the action | I, he, she, it, we, they | **She** waved. |
| Object: receives the action or follows a preposition | me, him, her, us, them | Sam saw **her**. |
| Possessive: stands for something belonging to someone | mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs | The red bag is **hers**. |
| Reflexive: points back to the subject | myself, yourself, himself, herself, ourselves | She taught **herself**. |

Use a subject pronoun as the subject: “**He** helped Mia.” Use an object pronoun after a verb or preposition: “Mia helped **him**”; “This is for **her**.” A useful check for joined pairs is to remove the other person: “Mia and **I** went” becomes “**I** went”; “Dad drove Mia and **me**” becomes “Dad drove **me**.”

Distinguish possessive pronouns from possessive determiners: “That coat is **mine**” (pronoun); “That is **my coat**” (determiner). *His* can work both ways. Do not confuse *its* (belonging to it) with *it's* (it is or it has). The possessive form *its* normally comes before a noun, as in “the cat licked **its paw**.”

In “Ava told Lily **she** had won,” *she* could refer to either girl. Rewrite the sentence to make the meaning clear. Pronouns avoid repetition only when their reference is clear.

**Try it:** Choose *He/Him* in “___ carried the bags” and *we/us* in “The teacher spoke to ___.” Why could “Ravi told Omar he was late” be unclear?

## 6. Prepositions: showing relationships

A **preposition** connects a noun or pronoun to another part of the sentence, often showing position, direction or time. It commonly begins a **prepositional phrase**: *under the table*, *across the bridge*, *before lunch*. The noun or pronoun after it is its object in these examples.

| Relationship | Examples | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Position | under, beside, between, behind, above | The ball is **under the chair**. |
| Direction or movement | to, into, through, across, towards | We walked **across the bridge**. |
| Time | before, after, during, until | We ate **after the match**. |

Position and direction differ: “The bird sat **on the wall**” describes position; “The bird flew **onto the wall**” describes movement. After a preposition, use an object pronoun: “behind **me**,” not *behind I*.

The same written word may have another job. In “We ate **before the show**,” *before* is followed by a noun phrase and works as a preposition. In “We ate **before the show began**,” it introduces a clause and works as a conjunction. In “We waited **before**,” it works as an adverb.

**Try it:** Find the prepositional phrase in “The ball rolled under the table.” Is *into* about position or direction in “The fox ran into the woods”?

## 7. Conjunctions: joining words and ideas

A **conjunction** joins words, phrases or clauses. **Coordinating conjunctions** join equal parts: *and* adds, *but* contrasts, and *or* offers a choice.

- Words: “tea **or** juice.”
- Phrases: “in the garden **and** under the tree.”
- Main clauses: “The bell rang, **and** the children left.”

When two main clauses are joined, each can stand alone. But “The dog barked **and** jumped” has one shared subject, so *and* does not automatically make it two clauses.

**Subordinating conjunctions** introduce a clause that depends on another clause. The joining word signals the relationship:

| Word | Relationship | Example |
|---|---|---|
| because | reason | We stayed home **because** it rained. |
| although | contrast | We walked **although** it rained. |
| when | time | We left **when** the film ended. |
| if | condition | We will leave **if** the film ends early. |

Choose a conjunction that matches the intended meaning. “She carried an umbrella **because** it was raining” gives a reason; “She carried an umbrella **although** it was sunny” expresses a contrast.

**Try it:** Choose *because* or *although*: “We took coats ___ it was cold.” Is *but* coordinating or subordinating in “I looked, but I could not see”?

## 8. Determiners: specifying nouns

A **determiner** comes before a noun (and before its adjectives) to specify which one, whose, or how many/how much: **the** small dog; **those** three red balloons.

| Group | Examples | What it tells us |
|---|---|---|
| Articles | a, an, the | One unspecified item or an identified item |
| Demonstratives | this, that, these, those | Which near or distant item(s) |
| Quantifiers | some, many, few, every, several | Amount or number |
| Possessive determiners | my, your, his, her, its, our, their | Whose |

Match demonstratives to number: **this** book, **these** books, **that** tree, **those** trees. Use *many* with countable things (“many apples”) and *much* with an amount (“much water”); *some* can work with either.

### Articles: a, an, the and no article

Articles are determiners. Use **a/an** for one unspecified singular countable noun: “I saw **a** bird.” Choose between *a* and *an* by the **first sound** of the next word: **a** university (the first sound is *y*); **an** hour (the *h* is silent). If an adjective comes first, listen to that: **an old** house, **a large** house.

Use **the** when the noun is particular or identifiable: “I saw **a** dog. **The** dog had a red collar.” Sometimes use no article when speaking generally: “**Dogs** need exercise”; “**Water** is important.” Compare with identified examples: “**The dogs** in our garden need exercise”; “**The water** in this bottle is cold.” Some proper names take *the*, including *the United Kingdom*.

**Determiner or pronoun?** “**Those books** are mine” uses *those* as a determiner before a noun. “**Those** are mine” uses it as a pronoun without a following noun.

**Try it:** Choose *a/an*: “___ useful tool”; “___ hour later.” In “I saw ___ fox. ___ fox ran away,” complete both gaps.

## 9. Interjections: expressing a reaction

An **interjection** is a short expression of reaction or emotion: **Oh! Wow! Hurray! Ouch! Alas!** Context and punctuation affect how it sounds. “Oh!” may show surprise; “Oh.” may show quiet realisation. An exclamation mark does not make every word an interjection: “Stop!” is a command verb in “Stop! The road is busy.”

An interjection is usually not needed for the rest of the sentence to work: “**Wow!** The rainbow is bright.” The sentence “The rainbow is bright” is still complete without *Wow!*.

**Try it:** Identify the interjection in “Hurray! Our team won.” Is “Stop!” an interjection or a command verb in “Stop! The road is busy”?

## Common traps

- **Adjective or adverb:** An adjective describes a noun (“the **quick** fox”); an adverb can describe a verb (“the fox ran **quickly**”). With a linking verb, use an adjective: “The flowers smell **sweet**.”
- **A word's job depends on context:** *Run* can be a noun or verb; *before* can be a preposition, conjunction or adverb.
- **Pronoun reference:** Check that the reader can identify what a pronoun refers to.
- **Determiner or pronoun:** A determiner comes before a noun (“those books”); a pronoun can stand in its place (“those are mine”).
- **Articles:** Choose *a/an* by sound, not spelling (“a university,” “an hour”).
- **Interjection or command:** “Ouch!” is an interjection; “Stop!” can be a verb giving a command.

## Teaching activity

Choose a paragraph from a story. Ask the child to find nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs, then identify a pronoun, preposition, conjunction and determiner. Discuss what changes if a word is removed or replaced. Focus on explaining each word's job, not just naming it.
