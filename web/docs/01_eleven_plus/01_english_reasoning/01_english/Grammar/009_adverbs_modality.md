# G. Adverbs and Modality

## 20. Adverbs

### What is an adverb?

An **adverb** adds information to a sentence. It often tells us more about a **verb** (an action), but it can also add information to an **adjective** or another **adverb**.

Compare:

- She spoke **quietly**. — Quietly tells us how she spoke.
- The water was **very** cold. — Very makes the adjective cold stronger.
- He ran **really** quickly. — Really strengthens the adverb quickly.

**Teaching tip:** Ask, “What does this word tell us or change in this sentence?” Do not identify an adverb by its spelling alone.

### Five useful meaning groups

| Type of adverb | Question | Examples | Example sentence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Manner** | How? | gently, quickly, well, carefully | She closed the door **gently**. |
| **Time** | When? | now, yesterday, later, soon, tomorrow | We will meet **later**. |
| **Place** | Where? | here, outside, nearby, upstairs | Please wait **outside**. |
| **Frequency** | How often? | always, often, sometimes, rarely, never | He **often** reads at night. |
| **Possibility or certainty** | How sure is the speaker? | perhaps, probably, certainly | She will **probably** arrive. |

These groups are based on meaning. A sentence can contain several adverbs at once.

**Example:** “**Yesterday**, Maya **carefully** placed her bag **outside**.”

- Yesterday tells us when.
- Carefully tells us how.
- Outside tells us where.

### Where can an adverb go in a sentence?

Adverbs can appear in different positions. Do not decide that a word is an adverb simply because it is at the end of a sentence.

- **At the beginning:** **Later**, we played chess.
- **At the end:** We played chess **later**.
- **Before an ordinary verb:** He **often reads**.
- **After the verb be:** He **is often** late.
- **At the beginning or within a sentence:** **Perhaps** she left. / She **perhaps** left.

The position can vary, although some arrangements sound more natural than others.

### The ending -ly is a clue, not a rule

Many adverbs end in *-ly*, but not all words ending in *-ly* are adverbs, and not all adverbs end in *-ly*.

| Word | How it is used | Word type |
|---|---|---|
| quietly | He spoke **quietly**. It tells us how he spoke. | Adverb |
| friendly | A **friendly** neighbour. It describes the noun neighbour. | Adjective |
| fast | A **fast** train. It describes the train. | Adjective |
| fast | The train moved **fast**. It tells us how the train moved. | Adverb |
| early | An **early** train. It describes the train. | Adjective |
| early | We arrived **early**. It tells us when we arrived. | Adverb |

**Worked example:** “The **friendly** guide spoke **clearly**.”

- Friendly describes the noun guide, so it is an adjective.
- Clearly tells us how the guide spoke, so it is an adverb.

**Remember:** The job a word does in its sentence matters more than its ending.

### Adverb or preposition?

Some words can do different jobs in different sentences. Look at what comes after the word.

- “We played **outside**.” — Outside tells us where we played. It is an adverb.
- “We played **outside the house**.” — Outside is followed by the noun phrase the house, so it is a preposition.
- “We left **afterwards**.” — Afterwards tells us when we left. It is a time adverb.
- “We left **after lunch**.” — After is followed by the noun phrase lunch, so it is a preposition.

You will learn more about these differences in Topic 23.

### Why does choosing an adverb matter?

Adverbs can change the picture a reader forms in their mind.

- He **carefully** opened the box. — He took care.
- He **eagerly** opened the box. — He was excited to see what was inside.
- She **rarely** visits. — She does not visit often.
- She **often** visits. — She visits frequently.

Carefully and eagerly both tell us how, but they create different impressions. When asked about an adverb, try to explain the effect it has, not just name its type.

## 21. Modal verbs

### What is a modal verb?

A **modal verb** is a helping verb that comes before another verb and changes how we understand the action or event.

Common modal verbs include:

**can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would, must**

Examples:
- She **might arrive**.
- You **must stop**.
- I **can swim**.

A modal can show possibility, expectation, prediction, obligation, advice, permission or ability.

### What can modal verbs mean?

| Meaning | Example | What it tells us |
|---|---|---|
| **Possibility** | It **might** rain. | Rain is possible; the speaker is unsure. |
| **Expectation or likelihood** | The bus **should** arrive soon. | The speaker expects it, but does not guarantee it. |
| **Strong prediction** | The bus **will** arrive at noon. | The speaker presents this confidently. |
| **Obligation** | You **must** wear a seat belt. | It is required. |
| **Advice** | You **should** take a coat. | The speaker recommends it. |
| **Permission** | You **may** leave. | The speaker allows it. |
| **Ability** | I **can** swim. | The speaker is able to do it. |

### One modal verb can have more than one meaning

**Use the whole sentence to work out the meaning.** A modal does not always have just one meaning.

**Must**
- “You **must** wear a helmet.” — Obligation: it is required.
- “You **must** be exhausted after that walk.” — Strong inference: I am almost certain you are exhausted.

**May**
- “You **may** leave now.” — Permission: you are allowed to leave.
- “It **may** rain.” — Possibility: rain could happen.

**Should**
- “You **should** practise.” — Advice: I recommend practising.
- “The train **should** arrive at six.” — Expectation: that is what the speaker expects.

### Comparing the strength of a prediction

Imagine we are talking about the weather:

- “It **might** snow.” — Snow is possible, but the speaker is unsure.
- “It **will** snow.” — The speaker makes a confident prediction.

Be careful: should and must are not simply steps on one certainty scale. Should may give advice or express an expectation. Must may express an obligation or a strong inference.

Before deciding what a modal means, ask:

**“Is the speaker predicting, advising, allowing, or requiring?”**

| Situation | Sentence | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Weather | It **might** snow. | Possible, uncertain. |
| Weather | It **will** snow. | Confident prediction. |
| House rule | You **may** use the computer. | Permission. |
| House rule | You **must** switch it off at eight. | Obligation. |
| Advice | You **should** check your answer. | Recommendation. |

### Modal verbs: grammar rules

#### Rule 1: Use the base form of the next verb

The **base form** is the basic form of the verb, without *-s*, *-ed* or *-ing*.

- Correct: She might **go**.
- Incorrect: She might **goes**.
- Correct: He should **listen**.
- Incorrect: He should **listens**.

#### Rule 2: Do not add -s to the modal for he, she or it

- Correct: He **can** run.
- Incorrect: He **cans** run.

#### Rule 3: Put not after the modal to make a negative

- She **might not come**.
- You **must not enter**.

#### Rule 4: Must not and do not have to mean different things

- “You **must not** go.” — Going is forbidden.
- “You **do not have to** go.” — Going is not necessary; it is optional.

This is an important difference. Do not treat the two expressions as interchangeable.

### Worked example: choosing between might and must

“The lights are off and no one answers the door. They **might** be away.”

- Might means being away is one possible explanation.
- “They **must** be away” presents a stronger inference: the speaker thinks they are almost certainly away.
- However, there could be another explanation, such as everyone being asleep or out in the garden.

A strong inference is a conclusion drawn from clues; it is not the same as a rule or obligation.

## 22. Adverbs of possibility and certainty

### What are adverbs of possibility?

Adverbs such as **perhaps, possibly, probably, certainly** and **definitely** tell us how sure the speaker is about an idea.

They are **adverbs**, not modal verbs. Both modal verbs and these adverbs can change how certain a statement sounds.

### A useful scale from less certain to more confident

| Adverb | Usual meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **perhaps** | Maybe; the speaker is considering a possibility. | **Perhaps** the key is in the drawer. |
| **possibly** | It could happen or be true. | She could **possibly** arrive early. |
| **probably** | The speaker thinks it is likely, but is not certain. | They will **probably** win. |
| **certainly** | The speaker expresses strong confidence. | I will **certainly** help. |
| **definitely** | The speaker expresses firm confidence or commitment. | We will **definitely** attend. |

This is a rough guide to everyday meaning, not a set of exact percentages. Perhaps and possibly often overlap. Certainly and definitely express confidence, but they cannot guarantee that a future event will happen exactly as predicted.

### What changes when we swap the adverb?

Compare these sentences:

1. “**Perhaps** the train is late.” — The speaker is considering lateness as a possible explanation.
2. “The train is **probably** late.” — The speaker thinks lateness is likely.
3. “The train is **definitely** late.” — The speaker claims to be sure.

The subject and basic event are similar, but the **degree of certainty** changes.

Ask: “What evidence would make this level of certainty reasonable?” If the departure board says *On time*, saying “The train is definitely late” is not well supported.

### Modal verb or adverb of possibility?

This distinction is important in grammar questions. Look at the word itself and the job it does.

| Sentence | Target word | Word type |
|---|---|---|
| The concert **might** start late. | might | Modal verb |
| The concert will **probably** start late. | probably | Adverb of possibility |
| The concert **might possibly** start late. | might / possibly | Modal verb / adverb |

A sentence can contain both a modal verb and an adverb. However, “might possibly” can sound repetitive in ordinary writing because both words express possibility.

**Exam tip:** If a question asks you to find a modal verb, do not choose probably. If it asks for an adverb of possibility, do not choose might.

### Worked example: more than one kind of word in a sentence

“**Perhaps** the parcel will arrive **tomorrow**.”

- Perhaps is an adverb of possibility; it shows uncertainty.
- Will arrive is the verb group, with will as the modal verb.
- Tomorrow is a time adverb; it tells us when the parcel may arrive.

A single sentence can contain different kinds of adverbs and a helping verb.

# H. Connecting Ideas of Time, Place and Cause

## 23. Connecting ideas

### Why do we need connecting words?

When we read a sentence or paragraph, we need to understand:

- **When** did something happen?
- **Where** did it happen?
- **Why** did it happen?
- Was there a **result**?
- Is there a **contrast** between ideas?
- Does one event depend on a **condition**?

Different words connect ideas in different ways. First work out the meaning you need, then check the grammar of the words that follow the connector.

### Three grammatical jobs: conjunction, adverb and preposition

Words that connect ideas do not all do the same grammatical job.

| Word type | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Conjunction** | Joins clauses or other units. A clause contains a subject and a verb. | We stayed in **because it rained**. (*It rained* is a clause.) |
| **Adverb / linking adverbial** | Adds information or links one sentence or event to another. | It rained. **Therefore**, we stayed in. |
| **Preposition** | Relates a following noun phrase to the rest of the sentence. | We stayed in **during the storm**. (*The storm* is a noun phrase.) |

### A useful test: what comes after the word?

Check what follows the connector:

- **Before lunch** — Before is followed by the noun phrase lunch, so it is a preposition.
- **Before we ate lunch** — Before is followed by the clause we ate lunch (subject we + verb ate), so it is a conjunction.
- **We ate before** — Before has no noun phrase or clause after it. Here it functions as an adverb.

This is a useful first test, although not every connector fits it perfectly. It helps prevent common mistakes.

### Conjunctions: learn the relationship they show

| Conjunction | Relationship | Example | Ask yourself |
|---|---|---|---|
| **because** | Cause or reason | We hurried **because** we were late. | Why did we hurry? |
| **when** | Time | We cheered **when** the race ended. | At what time did we cheer? |
| **while** | Overlapping time | She read **while** he cooked. | What happened at the same time? |
| **although** | Contrast | We played **although** it rained. | What happened despite the rain? |
| **if** | Condition | We will play **if** it stops raining. | What must happen for us to play? |
| **unless** | Negative condition | We will play **unless** it rains. | In what case will we not play? |

#### Because versus when

- “We went inside **because** the bell rang.” — The bell ringing is given as the reason.
- “We went inside **when** the bell rang.” — The bell ringing tells us when we went inside.

In real life, the bell might also be the reason for going inside. But the conjunction when itself expresses time, while because expresses reason.

#### If versus unless

- “You can go out **if** you finish your homework.” — Finishing your homework is the condition for going out.
- “You can go out **unless** it rains.” — You can go out except in the case that it rains.

Do not automatically replace unless with if not. Check the meaning of the whole sentence, especially if there are other negative words.

#### Although versus because

- “**Although** she was tired, she finished.” — Finishing was unexpected despite her tiredness. This shows contrast.
- “**Because** she was tired, she rested.” — Her tiredness explains why she rested. This shows reason.

### Linking adverbs: connecting events or sentences

These adverbs can link one event or sentence to another.

| Linking adverb | Relationship | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **then** | The next event | We packed. **Then** we left. |
| **afterwards** | A later event | We swam. **Afterwards**, we ate. |
| **meanwhile** | Events happening at the same time | Dad cooked. **Meanwhile**, Maya set the table. |
| **therefore** | Result or conclusion | It was raining. **Therefore**, the match was cancelled. |

#### Meanwhile versus therefore

- **Meanwhile** means the second action happened at about the same time as the first.
  - “Dad cooked. **Meanwhile**, Maya set the table.”
- **Therefore** signals a result or conclusion.
  - “It was raining. **Therefore**, the match was cancelled.”

Therefore does not simply mean later. “We ate lunch. Therefore, we opened the door” does not have an obvious logical link unless extra information explains it. Meanwhile is not suitable for an event that definitely happened hours later.

#### Punctuation with linking adverbs

When these words begin a sentence, a comma often helps the reader:

- **Meanwhile,** the others waited.
- **Therefore,** we changed the plan.

Punctuation may differ depending on where the word appears. The key point here is to understand the link; the full punctuation rules can be studied separately.

### Prepositions: position, movement and time

A **preposition** often comes before a noun or noun phrase and shows a relationship such as time or place.

| Preposition | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **before** | before breakfast | Earlier than breakfast |
| **after** | after school | Later than school |
| **during** | during the concert | Within the time of the concert |
| **beside** | beside the river | Next to the river |

#### Beside versus besides

These words look similar but have different meanings:

- **Beside** means next to: “The bag is **beside** the chair.”
- **Besides** can mean in addition to: “**Besides** tea, we have juice.”

#### During versus while

Look at what follows the word:

- “**During the concert**” — During is followed by the noun phrase the concert.
- “**While we listened**” — While is followed by a clause, we listened.

Say “while we listened”, not “during we listened”.

Other useful place and movement phrases include:
- **above the door**
- **beyond the bridge**
- **across the field**

The word **nearby** can also be a place adverb when it stands on its own: “We found a café **nearby**.”

### One event described in three different ways

Compare these sentences:

1. “We left **because the show ended**.” — The conjunction because introduces a clause giving the reason.
2. “The show ended. **Afterwards**, we left.” — The linking adverb afterwards shows sequence: one event followed another.
3. “We left **after the show**.” — The preposition after introduces the noun phrase the show and shows time.

These sentences may describe the same outing, but they do not make exactly the same claim. Because explicitly gives a reason; afterwards and after tell us about timing.

**Something happening after another event does not automatically mean it happened because of that event.**

### A reliable method for choosing a connector

When you have a gap to fill or need to choose the best connector, follow these steps:

1. **Identify the intended relationship:** time, place, reason, result, contrast or condition.
2. **Check what follows the word:** a clause with a subject and verb, a noun phrase, or a new sentence?
3. **Read the whole sentence aloud:** does it make sense?
4. **Test another possible word:** would it change the meaning?

### Worked choice

“We stayed indoors _____ the heavy rain.”

The words **the heavy rain** form a noun phrase.

- **During the heavy rain** tells us when we stayed indoors.
- **Because of the heavy rain** tells us why we stayed indoors.
- **Because the heavy rain** is incomplete in standard usage here. Because normally introduces a clause, such as **because it was raining heavily**.

The best answer depends on whether the question asks when or why.
