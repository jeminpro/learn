# G. Adverbs & Modality

## 20. Adverbs

An **adverb** often adds information to a verb, but it can also modify an adjective or another adverb. “She spoke **quietly**” tells **how**; “It was **very** cold” uses *very* to strengthen an adjective. To identify the word, ask **what it adds here**, rather than looking only for the ending *-ly*.

| Meaning group | Question | Examples | Sentence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Manner** | How? | gently, quickly, well | She closed the door **gently**. |
| **Time** | When? | now, yesterday, later, soon | We will meet **later**. |
| **Place** | Where? | here, outside, nearby, upstairs | Wait **outside**. |
| **Frequency** | How often? | always, often, sometimes, rarely, never | He **often** reads at night. |
| **Possibility/certainty** | How sure? | perhaps, probably, certainly | She will **probably** arrive. |

These are **meaning groups**, not five wholly separate parts of speech. A writer may use several in one sentence: “**Yesterday**, Maya **carefully** placed her bag **outside**.” *Yesterday* tells when, *carefully* how, and *outside* where.

### Adverbs can occupy different positions

“**Later**, we played chess” and “We played chess **later**” convey the same basic timing. “He **often** reads” usually places the frequency adverb before the ordinary verb; “He **is often** late” places it after *is*. “Perhaps she left” and “She perhaps left” show that position can vary, though some arrangements sound more natural than others. Do not decide a word's class just because it is at the end of a sentence.

### *-ly* is a clue, not a rule

*Quietly* is an adverb in “He spoke **quietly**,” but *friendly* is an adjective in “a **friendly** neighbour.” *Fast* can be an adjective (“a **fast** train”) or an adverb (“the train moved **fast**”). *Early* can be an adjective (“an **early** train”) or an adverb (“we arrived **early**”). Context decides.

**Worked example:** “The friendly guide spoke clearly.” *Friendly* describes the **guide** and is an adjective. *Clearly* describes **how the guide spoke** and is an adverb.

### Adverb or another connector?

“We played **outside**” has *outside* working on its own as a place adverb. “We played **outside the house**” uses *outside* with *the house* as a preposition. “We left **afterwards**” uses a time adverb. “We left **after lunch**” has a preposition plus a noun phrase. Topic 23 returns to these distinctions.

### Why does the choice matter?

“He **carefully** opened the box” suggests caution. “He **eagerly** opened the box” suggests excitement. Both words tell **how** but create different impressions. “She **rarely** visits” and “She **often** visits” change our understanding of frequency. Practise explaining the effect, not just naming the adverb.

## 21. Modal verbs

A **modal verb** is a helping verb such as **may, might, could, can, should, will, would, must**. It comes before another verb: “She **might arrive**”; “You **must stop**.” The modal affects how the speaker presents an event: as possible, expected, necessary, permitted, or something someone is able to do.

| What the speaker means | Example | A useful interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Possibility** | “It **might** rain.” | Rain is possible; the speaker is unsure. |
| **Expectation / likelihood** | “The bus **should** arrive soon.” | The speaker expects it, but does not guarantee it. |
| **Strong prediction** | “The bus **will** arrive at noon.” | The speaker presents this confidently. |
| **Obligation** | “You **must** wear a seat belt.” | It is required. |
| **Advice** | “You **should** take a coat.” | The speaker recommends it. |
| **Permission** | “You **may** leave.” | The speaker allows it. |
| **Ability** | “I **can** swim.” | The speaker is able to do it. |

### One modal can have more than one meaning

The **context** controls the meaning. “You **must** be exhausted” is a strong **inference** (“I am almost certain you are exhausted”), while “You **must** wear a helmet” expresses **obligation**. “You **may** leave now” grants permission, while “It **may** rain” expresses possibility. “You **should** practise” is advice; “The train **should** arrive at six” is an expectation. It is misleading to give each modal a single permanent percentage of certainty.

### Compare the strength without overclaiming

For the same context, “It **might** rain” leaves substantial uncertainty; “It **will** rain” presents a confident prediction. But **should** and **must** are not simply points on one universal certainty scale: *must* can mean obligation or strong inference, and *should* can mean advice or expectation. Ask **“Is the speaker predicting, advising, allowing, or requiring?”** before comparing strength.

| Situation | Sentence | What changes? |
|---|---|---|
| Weather | “It **might** snow.” | Possible, uncertain. |
| Weather | “It **will** snow.” | Confident prediction. |
| House rule | “You **may** use the computer.” | Permission. |
| House rule | “You **must** switch it off at eight.” | Obligation. |
| Advice | “You **should** check your answer.” | Recommendation. |

### Form and errors

A modal is normally followed by the **base form** of the verb: “She **might go**,” not “she might goes”; “He **should listen**,” not “he should listens.” It does not gain *-s* with *he/she/it*: “He **can run**.” For a negative, *not* may follow it: “She **might not come**”; “You **must not enter**.” **Must not** means prohibition, whereas **do not have to** means lack of necessity — a very different message.

**Worked choice:** “The lights are off and no one answers the door. They **might** be away.” This is a plausible possibility. “They **must** be away” presents a stronger inference, but there may be other explanations. Discuss what the evidence actually supports.

## 22. Adverbs of possibility and certainty

Adverbs such as **perhaps, possibly, probably, certainly, definitely** show how sure a speaker is about an idea. Unlike modal verbs, these are **adverbs**. Both types can affect the reader's sense of certainty.

| Adverb | Usual message | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **perhaps** | maybe; open possibility | “**Perhaps** the key is in the drawer.” |
| **possibly** | it could happen/be true | “She could **possibly** arrive early.” |
| **probably** | more likely than not, though uncertain | “They will **probably** win.” |
| **certainly** | strong confidence | “I will **certainly** help.” |
| **definitely** | firm confidence or commitment | “We will **definitely** attend.” |

These are **approximate everyday meanings**, not exact probabilities. *Perhaps* and *possibly* often overlap. *Certainly* and *definitely* can express a speaker's confident belief or commitment, not a guarantee that the future will unfold exactly as predicted.

### What changes when you swap the adverb?

- “**Perhaps** the train is late.” The speaker is considering it as an explanation.
- “The train is **probably** late.” The speaker thinks lateness is likely.
- “The train is **definitely** late.” The speaker claims to be sure.

The subject and basic event remain similar; the **degree of certainty** changes. Ask what evidence would justify each version. If the departure board says *On time*, “definitely late” is not well supported.

### Modal verb versus adverb

“The concert **might** start late” uses **might**, a modal verb. “The concert will **probably** start late” uses **probably**, an adverb. “The concert **might possibly** start late” combines both, but in ordinary writing it is often needlessly repetitive. If a question asks for a modal verb, do not select *probably*; if it asks for an adverb of possibility, do not select *might*.

**Worked example:** “Perhaps the parcel will arrive tomorrow.” *Perhaps* is the adverb signalling uncertainty; *will arrive* is the verb group. The word *tomorrow* tells **when**. One sentence can contain different kinds of adverbs and a helping verb.

# H. Time, Place & Cause

## 23. Connecting ideas of time, place and cause

To understand a sentence or paragraph, a reader needs to know **when** events happened, **where** they happened, **why** they happened, and sometimes whether one idea **contrasts with** or **depends on** another. Different kinds of words can make these links. Choose for **meaning first**, then check the grammar of the words around the connector.

### Three different grammatical jobs

| Job | What comes with it? | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Conjunction** | Joins clauses or other units | “We stayed in **because it rained**.” (*it rained* is a clause) |
| **Adverb / linking adverbial** | Adds information or links to another sentence | “It rained. **Therefore**, we stayed in.” |
| **Preposition** | Relates a following noun phrase to the rest | “We stayed in **during the storm**.” (*the storm* is a noun phrase) |

For a child, a useful first check is: **What comes after the word?** “**Before lunch**” has *before* + noun phrase: preposition. “**Before we ate lunch**” has *before* + clause (*we ate*): conjunction. “We ate **before**” has no following noun or clause: *before* functions as an adverb. Not every connector fits this quick test neatly, but it prevents many common errors.

### Conjunctions: compare meanings

| Word | Relationship | Example | Child-friendly explanation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **because** | Cause/reason | We hurried **because** we were late. | Why did we hurry? |
| **when** | Time | We cheered **when** the race ended. | At what time? |
| **while** | Overlapping time | She read **while** he cooked. | Both happened during the same period. |
| **although** | Contrast | We played **although** it rained. | It happened despite the rain. |
| **if** | Condition | We will play **if** it stops raining. | It depends on something happening. |
| **unless** | Negative condition | We will play **unless** it rains. | We will play except if it rains. |

**Because** answers *why*; **when** answers *when*. “We went inside **because** the bell rang” makes the bell the reason. “We went inside **when** the bell rang” locates the time; the bell may also be the reason in real life, but the conjunction itself expresses **time**.

**If versus unless:** “You can go out **if** you finish your homework” makes finishing the condition for going out. “You can go out **unless** it rains” permits going out except in the case of rain. Do not automatically replace *unless* with *if not* without checking the entire sentence's meaning and any other negatives.

**Although versus because:** “Although she was tired, she finished” presents finishing as unexpected despite tiredness. “Because she was tired, she rested” gives tiredness as the reason for resting.

### Adverbs that connect sentences or events

| Adverb | Usual link | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **then** | Next event | We packed. **Then** we left. |
| **afterwards** | A later event | We swam. **Afterwards**, we ate. |
| **meanwhile** | Simultaneous event elsewhere or with someone else | Dad cooked. **Meanwhile**, Maya set the table. |
| **therefore** | Result/conclusion | It was raining. **Therefore**, the match was cancelled. |

**Therefore** signals a **result or conclusion**, not just “later.” “It rained. Therefore, the match was cancelled” makes a logical cause-to-result link. “We ate lunch. Therefore, we opened the door” has no obvious logical link unless more information is supplied. **Meanwhile** means the second action happened around the **same time** as the first; do not use it for an event that definitely happened hours later.

When these words open a sentence, a comma often helps the reader: “**Meanwhile,** the others waited.” “**Therefore,** we changed the plan.” A paragraph can also use them within a sentence; punctuation may differ with placement and style. Your punctuation section can cover the full rules separately.

### Prepositions: position, movement and time

| Word | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **before** | before breakfast | Earlier than breakfast. |
| **after** | after school | Later than school. |
| **during** | during the concert | Within the time of the concert. |
| **beside** | beside the river | Next to the river. |

**Beside** means *next to*, while **besides** can mean *in addition to*: “The bag is **beside** the chair”; “**Besides** tea, we have juice.” A preposition links to a noun phrase: *during the concert*, not “during we listened.” Say “**while we listened**” if what follows is a clause.

Other useful location and movement links include *above the door, beyond the bridge, across the field*. In a separate sentence, *nearby* can work as a place adverb: “We found a café **nearby**.”

### A single event told three ways

Compare these sentences and ask what each connector makes clear:

1. “We left **because the show ended**.” A conjunction introduces a clause giving the **reason**.
2. “The show ended. **Afterwards**, we left.” A linking adverb shows **sequence**.
3. “We left **after the show**.” A preposition introduces a noun phrase showing **time**.

They may describe a similar outing, but they do not make identical claims: *because* explicitly gives a reason; *afterwards* and *after* give timing. “After this happened” does not automatically mean “because this happened.”

### Choosing the best connector: a reliable method

1. **Name the intended relationship:** time, place, reason, result, contrast or condition.
2. **Check the words that follow:** a clause with a subject and verb, a noun phrase, or a new sentence?
3. **Read the whole sentence aloud:** does the result make sense?
4. **Test a competing word:** would it change the meaning?

**Worked choice:** “We stayed indoors ___ the heavy rain.” *The heavy rain* is a noun phrase. **During** the heavy rain gives the time; **because of** the heavy rain gives the reason. “Because the heavy rain” is incomplete because *because* normally introduces a clause: “because **it was raining heavily**.” The correct answer depends on whether the question asks **when** or **why**.
