# Sentence Structure and Tenses

## Sentence structure and clauses

### First: what makes a sentence complete?

A sentence expresses a complete thought and normally contains a **subject** and a **verb**. “The cat slept.” is complete. “Because the cat was tired” leaves us waiting to hear what happened because of that tiredness.

A **clause** is a group of words with a verb that normally has its own subject. A **main clause** makes sense as a complete sentence: “The children went inside.” A **subordinate clause** depends on a main clause: “because it was raining.” Together: “The children went inside **because it was raining**.”

### Start with the building blocks

- A **word** is one item: *rain*.
- A **phrase** is a group of words that works together but does not, by itself, contain a complete subject–verb relationship: *under the old bridge*; *the large brown dog*.
- A **clause** expresses a subject–verb relationship: “**the dog barked**”; “**because the dog barked**.” Both contain *dog* and *barked*, but the second depends on something else because of *because*.
- A **complete sentence** expresses a complete idea. The main clause “**The dog barked.**” can be a sentence. “**Because the dog barked.**” leaves the thought unfinished: what happened because the dog barked?

**A nuance for the parent:** A command such as “**Stop!**” is a complete sentence even though *you* is understood rather than written. The “find a subject and verb” method works well for the ordinary examples in this lesson, but is a teaching aid rather than a definition with no exceptions.

### Main and subordinate clauses: a worked example

> “Because the path was muddy, the walkers turned back.”

1. Find verb groups: **was** and **turned**.
2. Split the ideas: “**Because the path was muddy**” and “**the walkers turned back**.”
3. Test whether each part can stand alone as written. “The walkers turned back” can. “Because the path was muddy” makes us ask **what happened?**
4. Therefore “the walkers turned back” is the **main clause**, and “because the path was muddy” is a **subordinate clause** giving the reason.

Move the subordinate clause and compare: “The walkers turned back **because the path was muddy**.” The basic reason and action stay the same. When the subordinate clause comes first, a comma normally separates it from the main clause.

### What does a joining word contribute?

The joining word is a **meaning clue**. Substitute different words and discuss how the message changes:

| Sentence | What the link means |
|---|---|
| We left **because** the hall was closing. | Reason: the closing caused us to leave. |
| We left **when** the hall was closing. | Time: the two events happened together. |
| We left **although** the hall was still open. | Contrast: we left despite it being open. |
| We will leave **if** the hall closes. | Condition: closing might lead us to leave. |

Choosing the right word is more valuable than simply memorising “conjunction.” “Although the hall closed, we left because it was closing” is grammatically possible but muddles the intended relationship; check the meaning as well as the structure.

### Three useful sentence patterns

| Pattern | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Simple | One main clause | **The dog barked.** |
| Compound | Two or more main clauses joined, often by *and, but, or* | **The dog barked, and the cat hid.** |
| Complex | A main clause plus a subordinate clause | **The cat hid because the dog barked.** |

**Do not count sentence length to identify the pattern.** “The very small, unusually noisy dog barked loudly at the gate” is still a simple sentence: it has just one main clause. Nor does every *and* create a compound sentence: “The dog barked and jumped” has one subject with two actions, so it remains one main clause.

### Tell the three patterns apart step by step

**Simple sentence:** “The tall, tired runner in the blue shirt **stumbled**.” There is one main clause, even though the subject has lots of detail. A simple sentence is not necessarily short or easy.

**Compound sentence:** “The runner **stumbled**, but her coach **caught** her.” Both “The runner stumbled” and “her coach caught her” can stand alone. *But* joins two equal main clauses and signals contrast. *And* adds an idea; *or* presents an alternative.

**Complex sentence:** “The coach **caught** the runner **when she stumbled**.” The *when* clause gives time and depends on the main clause. A complex sentence is not necessarily long or difficult. “I left **because I was tired**” is complex.

**Two useful tests:**

1. **How many clause-level subject–verb ideas are present?** “The dog barked and jumped” has one subject doing two actions in a single main clause. “The dog barked and the cat jumped” has two subject–verb ideas.
2. **Can both ideas stand alone exactly as written?** If yes, they can form a compound sentence: “The dog barked, and the cat jumped.” If one begins *because, when, although* or *if* and needs the other, the combination is complex.

| Sentence | Test | Type |
|---|---|---|
| The dog and the cat slept. | One main clause with a joined subject. | Simple |
| The dog barked and scratched the door. | One main clause with two actions. | Simple |
| The dog barked, and the cat hid. | Two main clauses. | Compound |
| The cat hid because the dog barked. | Main clause plus dependent reason. | Complex |

### Incomplete sentences and misleading punctuation

“**After the storm ended.**” has a capital letter and a full stop, but *after* makes the thought incomplete. Add a main clause: “**After the storm ended, we went outside.**” Conversely, “**The storm ended**” is a complete sentence even though it is short. Punctuation cannot rescue an unfinished thought.

Be alert to a **run-on**: “The sun set the stars appeared.” These are two complete ideas incorrectly pressed together. A clear repair is “The sun set, **and** the stars appeared” or “The sun set. The stars appeared.” For this pack, practise identifying and joining the clauses; detailed punctuation appears in section C of your wider checklist.

### Clause order and effect

Compare “**When the alarm sounded,** everyone left” with “Everyone left **when the alarm sounded**.” Both communicate the same time relationship, but the first introduces the time before the action. Read both aloud. Ask which part the writer wants us to notice first. Similarly, “**Although he was nervous,** Ali spoke clearly” highlights the surprise that he spoke clearly despite being nervous.

### How clauses join and what the link means

- **because** gives a reason: “We stayed inside **because** the storm began.”
- **when** tells us time: “We cheered **when** the team scored.”
- **although** shows an unexpected contrast: “**Although** it rained, we played.”
- **if** gives a condition: “We will play **if** it stops raining.”
- **and / but / or** can join two complete main clauses: “I wanted to leave, **but** Dad stayed.”

When a subordinate clause starts the sentence, a comma often marks the end of that opening clause: “**When the bell rang,** we packed our bags.” This comma also connects to your later section on fronted adverbials.

### Talk it through

Take “Although he was tired, Ben finished the race.” Ask: Which part can stand alone? (“Ben finished the race.”) Which part makes us wait for more? (“Although he was tired.”) What does *although* tell us about finishing despite tiredness?

### Try it

**4A.** Complete or unfinished? “When the lights went out.”  
**4B.** Name the pattern: “The sun set, and the stars appeared.”  
**4C.** Name the pattern: “The stars appeared after the sun set.”  
**4D.** Add a main clause: “Although the puzzle was difficult, ________.”  
**4E.** Name the pattern: “The excited children ran quickly to the park.”

### Answers

4A. **Unfinished**: the *when* clause needs a main clause. 4B. **Compound**: two main clauses. 4C. **Complex**: main clause plus an *after* clause. 4D. Example: “**Although the puzzle was difficult, we solved it.**” 4E. **Simple**: one main clause.

## Relative clauses

### The idea

A **relative clause** adds information about a **noun** already mentioned. It is usually part of a complex sentence.

> “The boy **who won the race** is my friend.”

*Who won the race* tells us **which boy**. The main clause is “The boy is my friend.” The extra clause helps identify him.

### Build a relative clause from two sentences

Begin with two ideas about the **same person or thing**:

> “I met a scientist. **The scientist** studies volcanoes.”

Replace the repeated noun with a relative word and attach the extra information to the noun:

> “I met a scientist **who studies volcanoes**.”

The extra clause describes **a scientist**. It helps us understand which scientist or adds information about that scientist. It does not describe *I* or the action *met*.

Try another: “We visited a village. **The village** lies beside a river.” → “We visited a village **that lies beside a river**.” Here the clause describes **village**. You can ask your child to draw an arrow from the clause back to the noun it describes.

| Opening word | Usually refers to | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **who** | a person | The girl **who found the key** smiled. |
| **which** | a thing or animal | The book, **which has a red cover**, is mine. |
| **where** | a place | This is the park **where we met**. |
| **when** | a time | I remember the day **when we moved**. |
| **whose** | belonging to someone | The boy **whose bag was lost** asked for help. |
| **that** | a person or thing in many identifying clauses | The coat **that I bought** is warm. |

### Each opening word in more detail

- **Who** refers to a person: “The doctor **who examined me** was kind.” Ask *which doctor?*
- **Which** commonly refers to a thing or animal: “The train, **which arrived late**, was crowded.” The clause supplies information about *train*.
- **Where** follows a place word: “The field **where we played** is now a garden.” The clause tells us about *field*.
- **When** follows a time expression: “The morning **when the snow fell** was unusually quiet.” The clause tells us about *morning*.
- **Whose** links something possessed or associated with someone: “The woman **whose bicycle broke** called for help.” The bicycle belongs to or is associated with the woman. *Whose* does not mean *who is*.
- **That** often identifies a particular person or thing: “The parcel **that arrived today** is for you.” In identifying clauses, *that* can sometimes replace *who* or *which*: “The child **that won**” and “The child **who won**” can both identify a child; *who* is often the more natural choice for people.

### Identifying information versus extra information

“The **book that has a red cover** is mine” may distinguish it from several books. Without the relative clause, the listener might not know which book. This is **identifying** information and normally has **no commas** around it.

“My **bike, which has a red frame,** is outside” tells us something additional about an already identified bike. The pair of commas sets off that extra detail. Compare the effect rather than teaching a blanket rule that every relative clause needs commas. Also, *that* is generally used for identifying clauses; use *which* for the comma-enclosed extra information in these examples.

### Relative clause or a different subordinate clause?

Both can begin with a familiar word. Ask what the clause does:

| Sentence | What does the clause add? | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| “I remember **the day when we moved**.” | **when we moved** describes the noun *day*. | Relative clause |
| “**When we moved**, I was excited.” | **When we moved** tells us when *I was excited*. | Time clause, not a relative clause |
| “This is **the park where we met**.” | **where we met** describes *park*. | Relative clause |
| “I smiled **because we met**.” | **because we met** gives a reason for smiling. | Reason clause, not a relative clause |

This is particularly helpful when a question asks **which noun** the relative clause describes. Do not decide solely from the word *when* or *where*; look for the noun it adds information to.

### Common errors and careful choices

- “The child **whose** coat was lost” is correct; “The child **who's** coat was lost” is not. *Who's* expands to *who is* or *who has*: “The child **who is** coat...” does not make sense.
- “The book **which I borrowed**” and “The book **that I borrowed**” are both natural identifying clauses. Avoid inventing a rule that only one can ever be correct.
- “The place **where we met**” and “The place **that we visited**” use different sentence patterns: *met* refers to a location; *visited* takes the place as its object.
- A clause may sit **inside** the main clause: “The boy **who won the race** is my friend.” To find the core statement, temporarily cover “who won the race”: “The boy is my friend.”
- Some identifying clauses omit the relative pronoun: “The cake **Mum baked** was delicious” = “The cake **that Mum baked** was delicious.” Do **not** omit it indiscriminately: “The cake **that fell**” cannot normally become “The cake **fell**” while keeping the same relative-clause structure.

**Worked identification:** “The author whose book we read visited our school.” The clause **whose book we read** describes **author**. The core sentence is “The author visited our school.” The clause tells us which author.

### Two questions to ask

1. **Which noun does the clause describe?** In “The cake **that Mum baked** was delicious,” it describes *cake*.
2. **What extra information does it give?** It tells us which cake was delicious.

### Choosing the right relative word

- A person: “the teacher **who** helped me.”
- A place: “the room **where** we practised.”
- A time: “the evening **when** we arrived.”
- Belonging: “the cyclist **whose** helmet broke.” *Whose* means belonging to whom; it is not *who’s* (“who is”).

**A useful extra:** Sometimes the relative word is left out: “The film **we watched** was funny” means “The film **that we watched** was funny.” This is useful to recognise, but start with clauses whose opening word is visible.

**Commas and meaning:** “My brother, **who lives in Leeds**, is visiting” gives additional information. “The brother **who lives in Leeds** is visiting” uses the clause to identify *which* brother. Do not put commas around every relative clause automatically.

### Talk it through

Compare “I saw a dog” and “I saw a dog **that had three legs**.” Ask: What information was added? Does it describe the dog, the seeing, or the speaker?

### Try it

**5A.** Find the relative clause: “The woman who painted the mural waved.”  
**5B.** Choose: “The house (where / whose) I grew up has a blue door.”  
**5C.** Choose: “The child (whose / who’s) coat was missing went back inside.”  
**5D.** Combine using *which*: “I found a shell. The shell was covered in tiny marks.”  
**5E.** Which noun is described by the relative clause? “The song that we heard yesterday was beautiful.”

### Answers

5A. **who painted the mural**. 5B. **where**. 5C. **whose**. 5D. “I found a shell **which was covered in tiny marks**.” 5E. **song**.

## Present and past tense

### The idea

The **tense** of a verb helps tell us **when** something happens. The **present** usually describes what happens now, regularly, or generally; the **past** describes something that already happened.

| Meaning | Present | Past |
|---|---|---|
| Habit / finished action | Mia **walks** to school. | Mia **walked** to school yesterday. |
| General fact / past state | The door **is** open. | The door **was** open. |
| Repeated / completed | They **play** chess on Fridays. | They **played** chess last Friday. |

**Time clues** such as *yesterday, last week, now* and *every morning* help, but the verb itself matters. A story written in the past can say “the next day” and still use past-tense verbs.

### Understand the verb before naming the tense

A **verb** can describe an action (*jump, whispered, drew*) or a state (*is, were, has*). To locate it, ask *“What is happening?”* or *“What is true about the subject?”* In “The door **was** open,” *open* describes the door and *was* puts that state in the past. In “Mum **has** a bicycle,” *has* is the verb even though nobody is visibly doing anything.

The **simple present** often expresses a routine or general truth, rather than an action taking place at this precise second:

- “I **brush** my teeth every morning.” This is a habit.
- “Water **freezes** when it is cold enough.” This is a general statement.
- “She **knows** the answer.” This describes a present state.

The **simple past** places an event or state earlier than now:

- “I **brushed** my teeth before breakfast.” A finished past action.
- “Water **froze** in the bucket last night.” A past event with an irregular verb.
- “She **knew** the answer yesterday.” A past state.

### How the past form is made

Many verbs are **regular** and form the past with *-ed*: *play → played; jump → jumped*. The spelling may change: *live → lived* (add *-d*); *try → tried* (consonant + *y* changes to *-ied*); *stop → stopped* (double the final consonant in this short stressed word). These are useful patterns, but the lesson's main purpose is choosing the correct form in context.

Some verbs are **irregular**. Group a few for practice rather than memorising a huge table at once:

| Present | Past | Present | Past |
|---|---|---|---|
| go | went | come | came |
| see | saw | say | said |
| eat | ate | give | gave |
| run | ran | take | took |
| write | wrote | think | thought |
| have | had | do | did |

**Worked choice:** “Yesterday the children (go / went) to the museum.” *Yesterday* points to the past, and the past of *go* is *went*, so choose **went**. “Every week the children (go / went) to the library.” *Every week* describes a routine, so choose **go**.

### Consistency means following the time line

In a past-tense account, connected events at the same past time normally use past forms: “Ella **opened** the box, **found** a note and **read** it.” “Ella **opened** the box, **finds** a note and **read** it” changes tense without a reason and makes the sequence awkward. To repair it, identify the shared time first, then check **each** verb.

A real change of time justifies a change of tense: “I **hated** olives when I was little, but now I **like** them.” The words *when I was little* and *now* explain the switch. A speaker's quoted words can have a different tense too: “Mia **said**, ‘I **am** ready.’” The past-tense reporting verb and present-tense speech are both appropriate.

### Common traps to discuss

- **Time word without the right verb:** “Yesterday he **goes**” is still wrong; the time word does not change the verb for us.
- **Over-correcting a meaningful switch:** “Last year I **walked** to school; now I **cycle**” is correct.
- **Confusing tense with spelling:** *Read* has the same written form in “I read every day” and “I read it yesterday,” but its pronunciation and tense differ in context.
- **Several verbs in one sentence:** “She **picked** up her bag and **runs** home” needs checking at both verbs if both actions happened yesterday.

**Explain-it question:** In “Tom **found** a coin, but now he **cannot** find it,” why are the two time frames different? A good answer points to the past discovery and his present difficulty.

### What to teach

1. **Find the action or state verb.** In “The children **were** excited,” *were* tells us their state.
2. **Match the verb to the time.** “Yesterday, I **went**” is correct; “Yesterday, I **go**” is not.
3. **Watch irregular past forms.** *Go → went; see → saw; eat → ate; run → ran; write → wrote; is/am → was; are → were.* These do not simply add *-ed*.
4. **Keep the time line clear.** “Yesterday, Sam **opened** the gate and **walked** inside” has a consistent past time frame.
5. **A tense change can be correct when time changes.** “Yesterday I **lost** my keys, but today I **have** them” deliberately moves from past to present. Do not teach that every sentence must use only one tense.

### Talk it through

“Every Sunday, Dad **cooks** lunch. Last Sunday, he **made** pasta.” Ask: Which phrase signals a habit? Which signals a finished event? Why is *made* right, even though it looks unlike *make*?

### Try it

**1A.** Underline the verb and name its tense: “The fox slipped through the hedge.”  
**1B.** Choose the correct word: “Each morning, Nina (packs / packed) her bag.”  
**1C.** Correct the accidental tense change: “Last night, Amir heard a noise and looks outside.”  
**1D.** Is this tense change sensible? Explain: “Last year, I lived in Bristol; now I live in Gloucester.”

### Answers

1A. **slipped**, past. 1B. **packs**. 1C. **looked** outside. 1D. **Yes**: *lived* refers to last year and *live* refers to now.

## Progressive forms

### The idea

A **progressive** verb form presents an action **in progress**. It has a form of **be** plus a verb ending in **-ing**.

| Form | Pattern | Example | What it suggests |
|---|---|---|---|
| Present progressive | am/is/are + -ing | She **is reading**. | Reading is happening now. |
| Past progressive | was/were + -ing | She **was reading**. | Reading was happening at a time in the past. |
| Simple present | ordinary present verb | She **reads** every night. | Habit. |
| Simple past | ordinary past verb | She **read** the note. | A past action stated as an event. |

**Important:** *Reading* on its own is not a full progressive verb in “Reading is fun.” Look for the helping verb *am, is, are, was,* or *were* with the *-ing* action.

### Build the verb in two pieces

Start with an ordinary verb such as *jump*. Its **-ing form** is *jumping*. Now place a suitable form of **be** before it:

| Subject and time | Helping verb | -ing form | Complete example |
|---|---|---|---|
| I, happening now | am | jumping | I **am jumping**. |
| He or she, happening now | is | jumping | She **is jumping**. |
| We or they, happening now | are | jumping | They **are jumping**. |
| I/he/she, happening in the past | was | jumping | He **was jumping**. |
| We or they, happening in the past | were | jumping | We **were jumping**. |

Both words belong to the verb form. If asked to underline the **whole** progressive form in “The players **were running**”, underline **were running**, not just *running*.

### What difference does the progressive form make?

Compare these pairs aloud and picture what is happening:

1. “Sasha **reads** after dinner.” This describes a **habit**. “Sasha **is reading** after dinner.” This presents a particular reading session as **in progress**.
2. “The dog **ran** across the garden.” This tells us that the event happened. “The dog **was running** across the garden when the gate opened.” This puts us **inside** the action as the gate opened.
3. “I **cooked** dinner and answered the door.” Two past events are mentioned. “I **was cooking** dinner when the doorbell rang.” Cooking was under way when a shorter event occurred.

The progressive form does **not** necessarily tell us that an action lasted a long time or whether it was eventually completed. It tells us how the writer presents the action at a particular time: as underway. “At noon, I **was crossing** the bridge” does not, by itself, say whether I reached the other side.

### Read the time line

Think of a long action as a line and a sudden event as a point on the line:

| Ongoing action | Event during it | Full sentence |
|---|---|---|
| was sleeping | alarm rang | I **was sleeping** when the alarm **rang**. |
| were playing | rain started | The children **were playing** when the rain **started**. |

In each example, past progressive (*was/were + -ing*) shows the action already underway; simple past reports the event that happened during it. Two actions can also be underway together: “I **was drawing** while my sister **was reading**.”

### Spelling and meaning traps

- *Make → making* drops the final *e*; *run → running* doubles the final *n*; *lie → lying* changes *ie* to *y*. Concentrate first on the two-part structure, then correct spelling.
- “The boy **running** down the road waved” contains *running*, but the main verb is *waved*; there is no *was running* or *is running* here. A word ending in *-ing* does not automatically make a sentence progressive.
- “They **were play**” is incomplete; use **were playing**. “They **was playing**” has the wrong helper; use **were playing**.
- “She **is being kind**” is a possible progressive expression, while a state such as “She **knows** the answer” is normally simpler than “She is knowing the answer.” Use clear action verbs when first teaching the pattern.

**Worked choice:** “At eight o’clock last night, we (watched / were watching) a film when the power failed.” The film was already in progress when the power failed, so **were watching** best expresses the intended relationship.

### What to teach

1. **Spot both parts:** “They **were waiting**” has *were* + *waiting*.
2. **Present versus past:** “I **am drawing**” is present progressive; “I **was drawing**” is past progressive.
3. **Simple versus progressive:** “Leo **kicked** the ball” reports the event; “Leo **was kicking** the ball” presents it as ongoing at a past moment.
4. **An action can interrupt one in progress:** “I **was reading** when the bell **rang**.” The reading was underway; the bell rang at a point during it.
5. **Choose a matching helper:** *He is running; they are running; she was running; they were running.* “They was running” is incorrect in standard English.

### Talk it through

Compare “The rain **fell**” with “The rain **was falling** when we arrived.” Ask: Which one helps you picture rain already happening at the moment of arrival?

### Try it

**2A.** Name the form: “The dog **is barking**.”  
**2B.** Name the form: “The dog **barked**.”  
**2C.** Choose: “At six o’clock yesterday, the children (were eating / eat) dinner.”  
**2D.** Complete with past progressive: “While the wind ________ (blow), the leaves ________ (fall).”

### Answers

2A. **Present progressive**. 2B. **Simple past**. 2C. **were eating**. 2D. **was blowing; were falling**.

## Subject–verb agreement

### The idea

The **subject** tells us who or what the sentence is about. The **verb** must fit that subject. In the present tense, a singular subject often takes a verb ending in *-s*, while a plural subject often takes the base form: **The bird sings. The birds sing.** This is a rule about verbs, not a rule that all words ending in *-s* are singular.

| Subject | Correct verb | Sentence |
|---|---|---|
| he / she / it / one child | is, has, does, runs | The child **has** a coat. |
| we / they / two children | are, have, do, run | The children **have** coats. |

### Find the real subject

In “The small brown **dog** **barks**,” *dog* is the subject and *barks* is the verb. The words *small brown* tell us about the dog, but do not change how many dogs there are. In “The small brown **dogs** **bark**,” the subject is plural and the verb changes. Ask **“Who or what is doing this?”** or **“Who or what is being described?”**

The **head noun** is the important noun in a longer subject. In “The **necklace** with three bright beads **is** mine,” *necklace* is the head noun. We have one necklace, even though we have three beads. In “The **beads** on the necklace **are** bright,” the head noun is now *beads*. Have your child cover up “with three bright beads” or “on the necklace” while choosing the verb.

| Longer subject | Remove the extra detail | Correct sentence |
|---|---|---|
| The **bag** of apples | The **bag** | The bag of apples **is** heavy. |
| The **apples** in the bag | The **apples** | The apples in the bag **are** fresh. |
| A **friend** of my parents | A **friend** | A friend of my parents **lives** nearby. |
| My **friends** from school | My **friends** | My friends from school **live** nearby. |

### Why does *-s* seem to move?

A plural **noun** often has *-s*: *bird → birds*. A present-tense **verb** often has *-s* with **one** person or thing: *one bird sings; two birds sing*. Children may incorrectly reason that because *birds* has *-s*, the matching verb must have *-s* too. Put the two columns side by side:

| One | More than one |
|---|---|
| The **bird sings**. | The **birds sing**. |
| My **friend plays**. | My **friends play**. |
| She **does** it. | They **do** it. |

This pattern applies to the third-person simple present, not every verb in every tense. “I **play**” and “you **play**” do not add *-s*. The past-tense verb *played* stays the same in “she played” and “they played.”

### Special verbs and longer verb groups

The verbs **be, have** and **do** are particularly common:

| One | More than one |
|---|---|
| He **is** ready. | They **are** ready. |
| She **was** ready. | They **were** ready. |
| The child **has** a pencil. | The children **have** pencils. |
| He **does** his work. | They **do** their work. |

In a progressive form, it is the **helping verb** that agrees: “The child **is playing**”; “The children **are playing**.” The word *playing* stays the same. Similarly, “The child **was playing**”; “The children **were playing**.” This links Topic 3 directly to Topic 2.

### Understanding *there is* and *there are*

In “There **are** three books on the shelf,” *there* introduces the sentence. Look ahead to **three books** to decide between *is* and *are*. Compare “There **is** one book” with “There **are** three books.” In conversation you may hear “there's three books”; for a formal grammar choice, **there are three books** is the standard match.

**Worked problem:** “The keys in the drawer (is / are) missing.” Ask “What is missing?” **The keys**. Cover “in the drawer.” “The keys **are** missing.”

**Avoid a doubtful example:** In British English, a collective noun such as *team* may sometimes take a singular or plural verb depending on whether the group is viewed as one unit or as its members. Use unambiguous subjects such as *box, child, players, keys* in an initial assessment.

### A reliable method

1. Find the **main verb**: “The box of pencils **is** heavy.”
2. Ask **what is heavy?** The *box*, not the pencils. *Box* is singular, so use *is*.
3. Ignore extra words between the subject and verb when choosing the verb: “The **players** on the field **are** ready.”

### Tricky patterns

- **There is / there are:** “There **is** a rabbit in the garden.” “There **are** three rabbits in the garden.” Look at what comes after the verb.
- **Two joined subjects:** “Ava **and** Tom **are** ready.” Both people together form a plural subject.
- **Past *was/were*:** “He **was** late”; “They **were** late.” Most ordinary past-tense verbs do not change this way: “He **walked**”; “They **walked**.”
- **A nearby plural can mislead:** “The **basket** of apples **is** full.” The basket is full; *apples* is inside an extra phrase.

### Talk it through

“The team of runners **is** ready.” Ask your child to point to the whole group named by *team*, then explain why *runners* does not control *is*. In everyday British English some collective nouns can also take plural agreement depending on meaning; for this exercise, use clear singular nouns such as *basket* or *box*.

### Try it

**3A.** “The girl with the red shoes (run / runs) fast.”  
**3B.** “The boxes beside the door (is / are) empty.”  
**3C.** “There (is / are) two answers.”  
**3D.** Correct: “The basket of oranges are heavy.”

### Answers

3A. **runs**; *girl* is singular. 3B. **are**; *boxes* is plural. 3C. **are**; *two answers* is plural. 3D. “The basket of oranges **is** heavy.”
