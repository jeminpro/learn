# Parts of Speech 
---

## 1. Nouns — naming people, places, things and ideas

A **noun** names a person (*doctor*), place (*park*), thing (*pencil*) or idea/quality (*courage*). It can be the subject (“**The cat** slept”) or part of another sentence element (“I saw **the cat**”). Try putting *a, the, my* or a number before it: *a pencil, the park, three pencils*. This test often helps, but proper names such as *London* do not normally need *a*.

| Type | Meaning | Examples | Sentence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Common** | General name | city, teacher, river | The **teacher** crossed the **river**. |
| **Proper** | Specific name | London, Aisha, River Severn | **Aisha** travelled to **London**. |
| **Collective** | Group named as a unit | team, choir, flock | The **choir** sang. |
| **Abstract** | Idea, quality, feeling or state | courage, kindness, fear | Her **courage** impressed us. |

### Understand each type

**Common versus proper:** *city* is a general category, while *Gloucester* names a particular city. Proper nouns usually have capital letters: *Monday, India, the River Severn*. Capital letters are the clue, but the reason is that the name refers to a specific person, place or entity. “My friend Aisha visited a city” contains a proper noun (*Aisha*) and common nouns (*friend, city*).

**Collective:** *team* means a group of players, but the word *team* is a single collective noun. *Players* is an ordinary plural noun, not the collective noun in “The team of players arrived.” Other examples include *class, audience, flock, bunch*. A collective noun does not have to take one particular verb in every British English sentence; focus first on what it names.

**Abstract:** You cannot usually hold *kindness* or *hope* as an object, but you can recognise and discuss them. A physical *medal* is a concrete noun; the *pride* someone feels about winning it is abstract. “The fear filled the room” uses *fear* as a noun despite describing an emotion.

### Words can change jobs

“A **run** around the park” uses *run* as a noun (a run); “I **run** around the park” uses it as a verb. “Her **kindness** mattered” has a noun; “She was **kind**” has an adjective. Always inspect the whole sentence.

**Worked example:** “The flock flew over Oxford.” *Flock* names a group, so it is collective. *Oxford* names a specific place, so it is proper. Nothing in the sentence makes *flew* a noun; it tells what the flock did.

### Try it

1. In “Maya showed great patience,” identify the proper noun and abstract noun.
2. Choose the collective noun: “A (flock / birds) gathered by the lake.”
3. Is *paint* a noun or verb in “The paint dried quickly”? How do you know?
4. Change the common noun *country* to one suitable proper noun.

**Answers:** 1. *Maya*; *patience*. 2. *flock*. 3. Noun: it names the substance that dried. 4. For example, *India* or *France*.

## 2. Verbs — actions, states and links

A **verb** tells us about an action, occurrence or state. It is central to a clause. In “The fox **jumped**,” it shows an action. In “The fox **is** hungry,” *is* connects the fox to a description. Verbs can change form to show time or agreement: *walk, walks, walked, walking*.

### Action verbs

These show what someone or something **does**: *run, lift, whisper, build, think*. Some actions are not visible: “She **remembered** the answer.” Ask “What did she do?” — *remembered*.

### Being and linking verbs

Forms of **be** include *am, is, are, was, were, be, being, been*. They may tell us a state: “The child **is** tired.” A **linking verb** connects a subject to a description or identity. “The soup **smells** delicious” links *soup* to *delicious*; it does not mean the soup is using its nose. “He **became** a doctor” links *he* to his new identity. Other common linking uses include *seems, appears, feels, looks, tastes*.

The categories overlap: **is** is both a form of *be* and a linking verb in “The room **is** quiet.” Some verbs can be action or linking depending on context:

| Sentence | Job of verb | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| The cook **tasted** the soup. | Action | The cook did the tasting. |
| The soup **tasted** salty. | Linking | *Tasted* links the soup to its quality, *salty*. |
| She **looked** at the clock. | Action | She deliberately used her eyes. |
| She **looked** tired. | Linking | *Looked* connects her to *tired*. |

### More than one word in a verb group

“They **were running**” has a helping verb *were* and a main verb *running*. “He **has finished**” has *has finished*. “She **might arrive**” has *might arrive*. If asked for the whole verb phrase, include the helping word; if asked for the main verb, name *running, finished* or *arrive*. This connects to the tense and progressive lessons in your A pack.

**Worked example:** “The flowers became dry.” *Became* links the subject *flowers* to the description *dry*. “The gardener watered the flowers.” *Watered* shows the gardener's action.

### Try it

1. Name the action verb: “The child solved the puzzle.”
2. Name the linking verb: “The sky grew dark.”
3. Is *felt* action or linking in “I felt the rough stone” and “The stone felt rough”?
4. Find the whole verb group: “The children were laughing.”

**Answers:** 1. *solved*. 2. *grew* (it links *sky* and *dark*). 3. Action in the first; linking in the second. 4. *were laughing*.

## 3. Adjectives — describing and comparing

An **adjective** gives information about a noun or pronoun. “A **heavy** bag” tells us about *bag*; “The bag is **heavy**” still uses *heavy* as an adjective, although it comes after the linking verb *is*. Adjectives can describe size, colour, shape, age, feeling or another quality: *small, red, round, ancient, cheerful*.

### Position and meaning

“The **quiet** room” and “The room **was quiet**” both describe the room. In “The **green** bicycle fell,” *green* describes *bicycle*, not the action *fell*. Ask “Which person or thing is it describing?”

Two adjectives can narrow the meaning: “a **small red** box” is more precise than “a box.” The adjective is not necessarily a single word ending in a particular set of letters. “A **wooden** bench” and “an **interesting** story” are both examples.

### Comparative and superlative forms

The **comparative** compares two: “Ali is **taller** than Ben.” The **superlative** picks the highest degree in a group: “Ali is the **tallest** in the class.”

| Basic | Comparative | Superlative | Pattern |
|---|---|---|---|
| tall | taller | tallest | add *-er, -est* |
| nice | nicer | nicest | add *-r, -st* |
| happy | happier | happiest | change consonant + *y* to *i* |
| big | bigger | biggest | double the final consonant |
| careful | more careful | most careful | use *more, most* |
| good | better | best | irregular |
| bad | worse | worst | irregular |

“More taller” usually doubles up the comparison; say **taller**. “Most tallest” doubles it too; say **tallest**. Some adjectives have acceptable alternatives depending on use (*cleverer/more clever*), so use clear examples when checking a rule.

### Adjective or adverb?

“The **quick** runner” describes a noun and uses an adjective. “The runner moved **quickly**” tells how the runner moved and uses an adverb. With linking verbs, keep the adjective: “The soup smells **wonderful**,” because *wonderful* describes the soup.

**Worked example:** “The second puzzle was harder than the first.” *Harder* compares the difficulty of two puzzles, so it is a comparative adjective. “The hardest puzzle on the page” identifies the highest degree among several.

### Try it

1. Identify two adjectives: “The tiny silver key opened the door.”
2. Complete: “This box is (heavy) ______ than that box.”
3. Complete: “Of the three roads, this is the (wide) ______.”
4. Choose: “The flowers smell (sweet / sweetly).” Explain.

**Answers:** 1. *tiny, silver*. 2. *heavier*. 3. *widest*. 4. *sweet*: it describes the flowers after the linking verb *smell*.

## 4. Adverbs — adding detail about an action or more

An **adverb** often adds information to a verb: “She spoke **quietly**” tells **how** she spoke. Adverbs can also modify an adjective (“**very** cold”) or another adverb (“**quite** slowly”). The four groups below are useful meaning groups, not separate parts of speech.

| Kind | Question | Examples | Sentence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Manner** | How? | quietly, carefully, well | She painted **carefully**. |
| **Place** | Where? | here, outside, nearby | Wait **here**. |
| **Time** | When? | now, yesterday, soon | We will leave **soon**. |
| **Frequency** | How often? | always, often, sometimes, never | I **often** read before bed. |

### Recognise the job, not just the ending

Many manner adverbs end in *-ly*: *slowly, happily, gently*. But **often, here, soon** are also adverbs, and not every *-ly* word is an adverb. “A **friendly** neighbour” uses *friendly* as an adjective describing *neighbour*. The question *what does this word add?* is more reliable than the ending.

An adverb may move: “**Yesterday**, we played chess” and “We played chess **yesterday**” both tell when. Some frequency adverbs commonly come before the main verb: “She **often visits** her aunt,” but after *be*: “She **is often** early.”

### Adverb versus preposition

“The children waited **outside**” uses *outside* on its own to tell where, so it functions as an adverb. “The children waited **outside the shop**” uses *outside* with *the shop* to show a relationship, so it functions as a preposition. “We left **before**” versus “We left **before lunch**” works similarly. The same written word can do a different job.

### Other useful adverbs

“The water was **very** cold” uses *very* to strengthen the adjective *cold*. “She ran **quite** quickly” uses *quite* to change the degree of the adverb *quickly*. In your wider FSCE list, *perhaps, probably, certainly* express degrees of possibility; these are also adverbs.

**Worked example:** “Yesterday, Aisha carefully placed the box here.” *Yesterday* = time; *carefully* = manner; *here* = place. All three help us understand the placing in different ways.

### Try it

1. Classify *silently* in “The fox moved silently.”
2. Classify *nearby* in “A guard stood nearby.”
3. Classify *rarely* in “We rarely eat out.”
4. Is *friendly* an adjective or adverb in “The friendly guide smiled”?

**Answers:** 1. Manner. 2. Place. 3. Frequency. 4. Adjective: it describes *guide*.

## 5. Pronouns — referring to people and things

A **pronoun** can refer to a noun or noun phrase without repeating it: “**Lucy** found a key. **She** kept **it** safe.” *She* refers to Lucy; *it* refers to the key. A reader must be able to identify the **referent** — who or what a pronoun points to.

| Job | Singular examples | Plural examples | Sentence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Subject**: does the action | I, you, he, she, it | we, you, they | **She** waved. |
| **Object**: receives action / follows a preposition | me, you, him, her, it | us, you, them | Sam saw **her**. |
| **Possessive**: stands for something belonging to someone | mine, yours, his, hers, its* | ours, yours, theirs | The red bag is **hers**. |
| **Reflexive**: points back to the subject | myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself | ourselves, yourselves, themselves | She taught **herself**. |

\*The possessive form **its** usually works **before a noun** (“the cat licked **its paw**”), so it is better taught with possessive determiners than as a stand-alone possessive pronoun. The stand-alone group is *mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs*.

### Subject versus object

Use a **subject pronoun** in the subject position: “**He** helped Mia.” Use an **object pronoun** where the action lands: “Mia helped **him**.” After a preposition, choose an object form: “This is for **her**,” not “for she.”

For a joined pair, temporarily remove the other person: “**Mia and I** went home” → “**I** went home” works. “Dad drove **Mia and me** home” → “Dad drove **me** home” works. This simple removal check prevents “Me and Mia went” and “Dad drove Mia and I.”

### Possessive pronouns versus possessive determiners

“That coat is **mine**” uses *mine* on its own: a possessive **pronoun**. “That is **my coat**” uses *my* before a noun: a possessive **determiner**. Likewise: *your/yours; her/hers; our/ours; their/theirs*. *His* can work in both places: “his coat” and “the coat is his.” Do not confuse **its** (belonging to it) with **it's** (it is or it has).

### Reflexive pronouns

“Nora taught **herself** to juggle” means Nora did the teaching and was also the learner. “I made the card **myself**” emphasises who made it. Do not use *myself* simply to sound formal: “Please send it to Dad or **me**,” not “Dad or myself” when *I* am not also the subject doing the action back to myself.

### Clear reference matters

“Ava told Lily **she** had won” is unclear: which girl won? Rewrite: “Ava told Lily, ‘**I** have won’” or “Ava told Lily that **Lily** had won,” depending on the intended meaning. Pronouns help avoid repetition only when the reader can track them.

**Worked example:** “Tom gave his sister a book, and she thanked him for it.” *She* = sister; *him* = Tom; *it* = book. Ask your child to draw an arrow from each pronoun to its referent.

### Try it

1. Choose: “(He / Him) carried the bags.”
2. Choose: “The teacher spoke to (we / us).”
3. Choose: “That blue pencil is (my / mine).”
4. Choose: “Amira looked at (herself / her) in the mirror” if Amira saw her own reflection.
5. Why is “Ravi told Omar he was late” potentially unclear?

**Answers:** 1. *He*. 2. *us*. 3. *mine*. 4. *herself*. 5. *He* might refer to Ravi or Omar.

## 6. Prepositions — showing relationships

A **preposition** connects a noun or pronoun to another part of the sentence, commonly showing **where**, **which direction**, or **when**. It usually begins a **prepositional phrase**: “under **the table**,” “across **the bridge**,” “before **lunch**.” The noun or pronoun after it is its object in these examples.

| Relationship | Examples | Sentence and meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Position** | under, beside, between, behind, above | The ball is **under the chair**. Where? |
| **Direction / movement** | to, into, through, across, towards | We walked **across the bridge**. Which route? |
| **Time** | before, after, during, until | We ate **after the match**. When? |

### Position and direction are different

“The bird sat **on the wall**” describes a position. “The bird flew **onto the wall**” describes movement to that position. “The dog was **in the shed**” versus “The dog ran **into the shed**” makes the same distinction. “We crossed **the road**” has *crossed* as a verb; “We walked **across the road**” has *across* as a preposition.

### Build and identify a prepositional phrase

“The letter **on the table** is yours.” *On* starts a phrase linked to *table* and tells us which letter. “We waited **until sunset**.” *Until* starts a phrase showing time. “The cat hid **behind me**.” After a preposition we use the object pronoun *me*, not *I*.

### The same word can do a different job

“We ate **before the show**” has *before* + a noun phrase (*the show*): preposition. “We ate **before the show began**” has *before* introducing a clause with a subject and verb (*the show began*): it works as a subordinating conjunction. This is why identifying a word from a memorised list alone may fail.

**Worked example:** “After dinner, the children ran through the garden.” *After dinner* tells **when**; *through the garden* tells **where/how they moved**. Both begin with prepositions.

### Try it

1. Find the preposition: “The shoes are beneath the bed.”
2. Is *into* about position or direction in “The fox ran into the woods”?
3. Find the time phrase: “We will leave before sunrise.”
4. In “We waited before,” is *before* followed by a noun/pronoun? What job does it do here?

**Answers:** 1. *beneath*. 2. Direction/movement. 3. *before sunrise*. 4. No; here *before* functions as an adverb meaning earlier.

## 7. Conjunctions — joining ideas

A **conjunction** joins words, phrases or clauses. The two groups in this lesson help explain the shape and meaning of a sentence.

### Coordinating conjunctions: joining equals

**And** adds, **but** contrasts, and **or** offers a choice:

- Words: “tea **or** juice” (two nouns).
- Phrases: “in the garden **and** under the tree” (two phrases).
- Main clauses: “The bell rang, **and** the children left” (two complete ideas).

When two main clauses are joined, each can stand alone: “The bell rang.” “The children left.” These form a **compound sentence** together. But “The dog barked **and** jumped” has a shared subject and is not automatically two main clauses. Test the structure, not just the presence of *and*.

### Subordinating conjunctions: one clause depends on another

**Because** gives a reason: “We stayed home **because it rained**.” **Although** introduces contrast: “We walked **although it rained**.” **When** marks time: “We left **when the film ended**.” **If** introduces a condition: “We will leave **if the film ends early**.” The beginning word makes “because it rained” or “although it rained” depend on a main clause.

| Word | Relationship | Example |
|---|---|---|
| and | addition | The door opened, **and** we entered. |
| but | contrast between equal clauses | I was tired, **but** I finished. |
| or | alternative | We can read **or** play chess. |
| because | reason | I rested **because** I was tired. |
| although | unexpected contrast | **Although** I was tired, I finished. |

**Because** and **although** are not interchangeable. “She carried an umbrella **because** it was raining” says rain was her reason. “She carried an umbrella **although** it was sunny” says her action seems surprising in those circumstances. Discuss the intended meaning before choosing a word.

**Worked example:** “Although the road was narrow, the bus passed safely.” “The bus passed safely” stands alone; “Although the road was narrow” depends on it. *Although* shows that safe passage might have been unexpected. A comma commonly follows the opening subordinate clause.

### Try it

1. Choose *because* or *although*: “We took coats ___ it was cold.”
2. Choose *because* or *although*: “___ it was cold, we went swimming.”
3. Is *but* coordinating or subordinating in “I looked, but I could not see”?
4. Find the main clause: “When the bell rang, the lesson ended.”

**Answers:** 1. *because*. 2. *Although*. 3. Coordinating. 4. *the lesson ended*.

## 8. Determiners — introducing and specifying nouns

A **determiner** goes before a noun (and before any adjectives modifying that noun) to help specify **which one**, **whose**, or **how many/how much**. Compare “**the** small dog” with “small **the** dog”: the second order is wrong. In “**those** three red balloons,” *those* points to particular balloons; *three* gives a number.

| Group | What it tells us | Examples | In a phrase |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Articles** | Known/specific or one unspecified | a, an, the | **a** story; **the** story |
| **Demonstratives** | Which near/far item(s) | this, that, these, those | **those** books |
| **Quantifiers** | Amount or number | some, many, few, every, several | **some** water; **many** cups |
| **Possessive determiners** | Whose | my, your, his, her, its, our, their | **her** coat |

### Demonstratives and number

**This** book (one near); **these** books (more than one near). **That** tree (one farther away); **those** trees (more than one farther away). The near/far difference can be physical or in the discussion: “**This** idea seems better than **that** one.”

### Quantifiers and countability

“**Many** apples” fits things you can count. “**Much** water” fits an amount you do not normally count as individual waters. “**Some** apples” and “**some** water” can work with both. “**Every** pupil” refers to each member but normally uses a singular noun; “every pupils” is not the usual form.

### Determiner or pronoun?

“**Those books** are mine” has *those* before the noun *books*: demonstrative **determiner**. “**Those** are mine” uses *those* without the noun: demonstrative **pronoun**. “**My** bag is blue” has a possessive determiner; “The blue bag is **mine**” has a possessive pronoun. The word's job in the sentence is the key.

**Worked example:** “Those three old maps are ours.” *Those* identifies which maps; *three* states number; *old* describes the maps (adjective); *maps* is the noun; *ours* stands alone as a possessive pronoun.

### Try it

1. Identify the determiner in “Several children waited.”
2. Choose: “(This / These) shoes are wet.”
3. Choose: “There is (much / many) milk left.”
4. In “Those are the ones I want,” is *those* a determiner or pronoun? Why?

**Answers:** 1. *Several*. 2. *These*. 3. *much*. 4. Pronoun: it stands without a following noun.

## 9. Interjections — brief expressions of feeling

An **interjection** is a short expression of reaction or emotion: **Oh! Wow! Hurray! Ouch! Alas!** It may stand alone or appear at the start of a sentence. It can convey surprise, pleasure, pain, disappointment or a call for attention.

| Interjection | Possible feeling | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Wow! | surprise / admiration | **Wow!** That tower is tall. |
| Ouch! | pain | **Ouch!** I stubbed my toe. |
| Hurray! | celebration | **Hurray!** We won. |
| Oh. | realisation / disappointment | **Oh.** I see what happened. |

### Read the context and punctuation

“Oh!” may express surprise; “Oh.” might show a quieter realisation. The word alone does not always specify the exact feeling. An exclamation mark makes the expression strong, but **an exclamation mark does not turn every word into an interjection**: “Run!” is an imperative verb in a command.

An interjection is usually not needed to make the grammar of the rest of the sentence work: “**Wow!** The rainbow is bright.” “The rainbow is bright” remains a sentence. It is a useful expressive device, but should not dominate study time ahead of the more central word classes.

**Worked example:** “Ouch! That was hot.” *Ouch* expresses an immediate reaction; *was* is the verb in the following sentence; *hot* describes *that*.

### Try it

1. Identify the interjection: “Hurray! Our team won.”
2. Is “Stop!” an interjection or a command verb in “Stop! The road is busy”?
3. Give a possible feeling for “Oh no! I left my keys behind.”

**Answers:** 1. *Hurray!* 2. Command verb. 3. Worry, disappointment or frustration are sensible answers.

## 10. Articles — a closer look at *a, an, the* and no article

**Articles are determiners.** English has the **indefinite articles** *a* and *an* and the **definite article** *the*. Sometimes English uses **no article** before a noun. The choice tells a reader whether something is being introduced, identified or spoken of generally.

### *A* and *an*: one unspecified member

“I saw **a** bird” introduces one bird without saying which. “She is **an** engineer” identifies her as one member of that profession. Use *a/an* with a **singular countable noun**: *a whisper, an apple, a chair*. “A apples” and “an information” are not standard choices in those senses.

Choose *a* or *an* by the **first sound of the next word**, not simply its first letter:

| Use *a* before a consonant sound | Use *an* before a vowel sound |
|---|---|
| **a** dog; **a** university (*yoo* sound) | **an** egg; **an** hour (*h* silent) |
| **a** useful idea (*yoo* sound) | **an** honest answer (*h* silent) |

If an adjective sits before the noun, listen to the adjective: **an old house** but **a large house**. The sound immediately after the article controls the choice.

### *The*: a particular or already identifiable one

“I saw **a** dog. **The** dog had a red collar.” The first sentence introduces it; the second points back to the same dog. “Please close **the** door” works if both speaker and listener can identify which door. *The* works with singular and plural nouns and with uncountable nouns: *the book, the books, the water in this glass*.

**A whisper versus the whispers:** “I heard **a whisper**” introduces one unspecified whisper. “I heard **the whispers** from the next room” points to particular whispers and uses plural *whispers*. The contrast changes both **specificity** and **number**, so do not attribute the entire difference to the article alone. For a cleaner comparison, use “**a whisper**” versus “**the whisper**.”

### No article: speaking generally or using certain names

“**Dogs** need exercise” talks generally about dogs; “**The dogs** in our garden need exercise” identifies particular dogs. “**Water** is important” is general; “**The water** in this bottle is cold” identifies particular water. “We visited **London**” uses no article with that proper place name. There are named exceptions such as **the United Kingdom**; do not try to turn this into a universal rule about all proper nouns.

| Meaning | Example | Why |
|---|---|---|
| An unspecified singular countable thing | “I found **a** coin.” | Any one coin, first mention. |
| A particular thing | “**The** coin was old.” | The coin just mentioned. |
| A whole category, plural | “**Coins** can be old.” | Coins in general. |
| A general substance | “**Milk** is white.” | Milk generally. |
| A particular amount/substance | “**The milk** in the jug is warm.” | Identified milk. |

**Worked example:** “An owl sat on a branch. The owl turned its head.” *An* follows the vowel sound at the start of *owl* and introduces one owl. *A* introduces one branch. *The* then refers to the owl we already know about. *Its* is a possessive determiner, not an article.

### Try it

1. Choose: “(A / An) hour later, we left.”
2. Choose: “(A / An) useful tool can save time.”
3. Complete: “I saw ___ fox. ___ fox ran away.”
4. Choose *the* or no article for a general statement: “___ Elephants have trunks.”
5. Choose: “Please pass me (water / the water) in that blue bottle.”

**Answers:** 1. *An* (silent *h*). 2. *A* (*yoo* sound). 3. *a; the*. 4. No article: “Elephants have trunks.” 5. *the water*, because a particular bottle identifies it.
